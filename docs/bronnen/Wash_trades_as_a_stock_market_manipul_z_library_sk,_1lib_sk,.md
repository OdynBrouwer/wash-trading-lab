# Wash Trades as a Stock Market Manipulation Tool **Abstract**

This study empirically investigates the proﬁtability of one of the most

widely used trade-based manipulation tools, namely wash trading. Using

a unique account-level data set for the period 2003-2006 from the Istanbul

Stock Exchange (ISE), we generate a measure for the usage of wash trades

for each individual account and examine whether wash trading provides

excess returns for investors. Our empirical results reveal that signiﬁcant

numbers of investors perform wash trades. In addition, we analyze the

optimal percentage of wash trades at which investors maximize excess

proﬁts. We ﬁnd that having up to 10% of total trades as wash trades is the most proﬁtable range, with a 0.5% monthly excess return.

Keywords: trade-based manipulation, wash trades, market structure JEL Classiﬁcation: G11, G14, G20

## 1 **Introduction**

Capital market supervisors allocate signiﬁcant resources to detecting manip-

ulative behavior in stock markets. Stock market manipulation damages public

conﬁdence in capital markets because it distorts the fair pricing of securities

by creating artiﬁcial prices. Accordingly, manipulation signiﬁcantly lowers in-

dividuals' stock market participation (Giannetti and Wang, 2016). Diminished

investor participation and liquidity decrease eﬃciency in the securities markets,

preventing them reaching their potential, especially in developing economies.

Hence, understanding the structure and eﬀects of manipulative behavior is cru-

cial to designing mechanisms that will detect and prevent manipulation. In

this study, we investigate one of the most widely used trade-based manipulation

tools: wash trades. The SEC deﬁnes a wash trade as "a securities transaction

which involves no change in the beneﬁcial ownership of the security." 1 *,* 2

Manipulators conduct "wash trades" by trading a stock without any genuine

change in the ownership or by engaging in transactions that are small, but fol-

low within very short intervals. These transaction methods are used as part of

a pump-and-dump manipulation scheme. In a general pump-and-dump manip-

ulation scheme, manipulators need to sell existing shares at elevated prices and,

hence, seek to have a high number of normal investors competing on the bid

side of the market in order to maximize their proﬁt. Therefore, the most viable

explanation for engaging in individually irrational and manipulative transac-

tions such as wash trades is that these trades are used to attract the attention

of normal investors who have limited resources to choose among many options.

These trades are conducted for tax-saving purposes (Grinblatt and Keloharju, 2004). In this study, we follow the deﬁnition of the SEC and investigate "wash trades" in a stock market manipulation setting.

Barber and Odean (2008) report that investors are more likely to buy rather

than sell attention-grabbing stocks that may exhibit abnormal trading volumes,

extreme daily returns, or that have a recent announcement of information. In

this context, attention-grabbing manipulative trades can be regarded as a mar-

keting strategy of manipulators to promote the shares in their portfolios.

A typical manipulation scheme can be analyzed in three phases. In the ﬁrst

phase, the manipulator purchases a substantial number of shares, driving the

market price up (accumulation phase). In the second phase, the manipulator

attempts to direct the attention of uninformed traders to the stock by using var-

ious manipulative methods such as wash trades, matching orders and spreading

rumors. In the third phase of the scheme, the manipulator sells her existing

stocks to the uninformed traders who are eager to acquire information on the

ﬁrm's prospects. By conducting wash sales, a manipulator buys and sells the

same stock without changing the real ownership of these stocks in order to ar-

tiﬁcially create the appearance of an active trading environment and to direct the attention of some information seekers to this stock.

It is diﬃcult to ﬁnd an economic explanation for a rational investor to be a

part of a wash trade. In such a scheme, there is no actual change in ownership,

because the seller and the buyer are the same investor, although this investor

may pay transaction costs. Thus, an investor who conducts a wash trade would

have a non-positive outcome from the trade. However, by conducting wash

trades, a manipulator attract the attention of some information seekers to the market of the stock and aﬀect their investment decisions.

Manipulators use wash trades to artiﬁcially increase the daily volume of a

stock in order to mislead investors by creating the appearance of a liquid market.

Many investors may value this increase in the liquidity as a positive indicator

for the relevant stock. Gervais et al. (2001) show that unusually high trading

volume is followed by positive excess returns. Hence, manipulators might try to

create the illusion of unusually high trading volume by conducting wash trades.

In another set up, a manipulator initially gives a number of passive selling orders

for the stock and then matches her own orders by giving binding buy orders.

Thereby, a manipulator can increase the price of the stock without a genuine

change in the ownership of the shares. However in most cases, other investors

cannot recognize this and perceive this movement as a regular rise in the stock

price. Many types of investors, including intra-day traders, can be vulnerable

to this manipulation scheme. Cumming et al. (2011) mention wash trading as

one of the primary forms of volume manipulation in addition to churning. They

state that the impact of wash trades on volume is more pronounced than the eﬀect on a stock price.

The IOSCO (2000) report on market manipulation identiﬁes wash trades as

one of the major types of manipulative conduct. It is reported to be used in

both developed and emerging capital markets. Aggarwal and Wu (2006) present

142 trade-based stock market manipulation cases by analyzing Securities and

Exchange Commission (SEC) litigation releases between 1990-2001 in United

States. SEC's litigation announcements are published on their website and

exhibit many examples of wash trade manipulation. In one of these cases, while

ﬁling a complaint about a manipulation scheme in the market of AutoChina 3 shares, SEC notes that:

Imisiker and Tas (2013) analyze the manipulation cases in an emerging stock

fraudulent activities contain wash trades as a crucial part of the scheme.

Despite being used extensively by manipulators, there are no empirical in-

vestigation of wash trades in the literature. The main reason for the lack of

empirical studies is the unavailability of comprehensive trade-level data. An

empirical investigation of wash trades requires trade-level data with informa-

tion about the identities of the purchaser and seller in each trade. The CMB

provides access to a detailed trade level data set of the Istanbul Stock Exchange

(ISE) at its premises in Ankara, which can be used by researchers. 4 Importantly,

this data set contains identiﬁcation numbers of the buyers and sellers for each

trade. Hence, this data set allows us to identify trades in which the buyer and the seller are the same investor (i.e., wash trades).

## 2 **Literature** ** ** **review**

Aggarwal and Wu (2006) provide empirical evidence about the eﬀect of ma-

nipulation on stock price and volume by investigating manipulation cases pros-

ecuted by the SEC. They ﬁnd that manipulation leads to a rise in volatility,

liquidity, and returns of the stocks. In general, prices rise during the manip-

ulation scheme, but drop after the manipulation period. Comerton-Forde and

Putnins (2011) ﬁnd additional evidence by studying closing price manipulation

cases. They construct an index of probability and intensity of closing price ma-

nipulation using a sample of manipulation cases prosecuted by US and Canadian

prosecutors. Cumming et al (2012) show that closing price manipulation is con-

ducted in 22 stock exchanges around the world. Kadioglu et al. (2015) ﬁnd

empirical evidence about upward-oriented closing price manipulation in Borsa Istanbul.

Manipulators commonly use corners and squeezes to manipulate stock prices. 9

9 As described in Merrick et al (2005, 173) "A classic manipulative delivery squeeze in a bond futures contract takes place when a manipulator acquires a substantial long position in the futures contract and a sizeable fraction of its cheapest deliverable bond issue. The squeezer attempts to proﬁt by restricting the supply of the cheapest deliverable issue."

Allen et al. (2006) examine stock market and commodity market corners for

the period 1863-1980. They assert that large investors and insiders have mar-

ket power that may let them manipulate prices, and these manipulations with

corners lead to increases in volatility. Merrick et al. (2005) investigate manip-

ulation cases with a squeeze on the bond futures market. They conclude that

traders earn signiﬁcant proﬁts during diﬀerent phases of the squeeze.

A popular manipulation scheme is the "pump-and-dump" trading strategy.

Mei et al. (2004) show that an uninformed manipulator can use investors' be-

havioral biases to proﬁt from pump-and-dump strategies. Khanna and Sunder

(1999) argue that brokers conduct pump-and-dump schemes in the Indian stock

market. Zhou and Mei (2003) show that manipulative trading behavior ex-

ists in many emerging markets, including China. Khwaja and Mian (2005) use

daily data from the Karachi Stock Exchange (KSE). They conclude that almost

60% of all trades can be identiﬁed as a part of a pump-and-dump manipula-

tion scheme in the KSE. They also ﬁnd that manipulative brokers earn 50 to

90% higher annual returns, at the expense of outside investors. Using a similar

methodology, Imisiker et al. (2015) show that a signiﬁcant percentage of the

trades conducted by brokers at the ISE are consistent with the pump-and-dump

price manipulation scheme. They further conclude that brokers who conduct

more manipulative trades earn higher proﬁts. Leuz et al. (2017) examine in-

vestor participation in 421 pump-and-dump schemes identiﬁed by the German

supervisory authority, BaFin. They ﬁnd that 6% of investors invest in at least

one scheme and their average loss is 28%. Renault (2017) shows that manipu-

lators might use social media to conduct pump-and-dump schemes. Suspicious

online activity about stock recommendations cause large price increases followed by dramatic price reversals.

Lee et al. (2013) identify another type of manipulation, called microstructure-

based manipulation, which can be categorized as a subcategory of trade-based

manipulation. They show that the unique microstructure of the Korea Ex-

change provides an opportunity for manipulators to use spooﬁng orders that

have a very small probability of being executed and are given to mislead other

investors. They report that a manipulator can earn extra proﬁts of 67-83 ba-

sis points in a very short period. Neupane et al. (2017) examine trade based

manipulation in Indian IPOs. They ﬁnd that manipulated IPOs features a

pump-and-dump scheme. Kong and Wang (2014) examine order-based manipu-

lation in the Chinese stock market. They ﬁnd that manipulation aﬀects investor

behavior signiﬁcantly in the short term. Chaturvedula et al. (2015) analyze ma-

nipulation in Indian stock markets by examining the eﬀect of bulk trade sales

and purchases on stock prices. They ﬁnd a signiﬁcant impact of bulk trades on

the share prices with cumulative returns being as high as 7.49%.

As stated above, an extensive literature investigates the existence and impact

of diﬀerent stock manipulation schemes. However, empirical studies on wash

trades as a trade-based manipulation tool do not exist in the literature because

of data limitations. We contribute to the literature by providing empirical

evidence on the existence of trades consistent with wash trade manipulation

schemes. Additionally, we demonstrate that investors can earn signiﬁcant proﬁts

by implementing this scheme if they use moderate levels of wash trades.

## 3.1 **Istanbul** ** ** **Stock** ** ** **Exchange**

The Istanbul Stock Exchange (ISE) was founded in 1986 to provide a secure

and stable environment for trading in equities, bonds and bills, revenue-sharing

certiﬁcates, private sector bonds, and other capital market instruments. The

ISE is a public corporation operating as an autonomous and professional insti- tution. 10

The ISE operates through a computerized system. This system automati-

cally matches buy and sell orders on a price and time priority basis. The stock

trading activities are carried out in two separate sessions. For the sample period,

the settlement of securities traded in the ISE is realized by the ISE Settlement

and Custody Bank Inc. (Takasbank), which is the sole and exclusive central depository in Turkey.

The market capitalization of the ISE was 162.4 billion US dollars at the end

of 2006, which is similar to the Tel Aviv, Irish, Warsaw, Jakarta, and Santiago

Stock Exchanges. At the end of 2006, there were 316 companies listed on the

ISE. In 2006, the number of trading days was 250, with an average daily turnover

of 890.9 million US dollars. The average value of trades was 4.8 thousand dollars.

alternative wash value score variable is used, and the ﬁrst decile is still the most

proﬁtable. Hence, we conclude that wash trades are proﬁtable and an investor

can earn proﬁts by conducting wash trades up to 30% of all of transactions.

## 7 **Conclusion**

In this study, we use a unique data set to identify a well-known trade-based

manipulation tool, namely wash trades, and the proﬁts earned through those

trades in an emerging market, the ISE. The unique structure of the data set

allows us to identify wash trade transactions in which the buyer and seller are

the same investor. We conclude that signiﬁcant numbers of investors perform

wash trades, and that having up to 30% of the total trades as wash trades

provides excess proﬁts. Using wash trades up to 10% of total trades is the most proﬁtable range, with a 0.5% monthly excess return.
