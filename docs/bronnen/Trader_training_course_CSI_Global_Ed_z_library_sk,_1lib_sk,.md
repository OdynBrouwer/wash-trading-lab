## investment Industry Regulatory Organization of Canada (IIROC) and UMIR

The Canadian Securities Administrators' on-going initiative to create a harmonized framework for the competitive operation of all Canadian marketplaces resulted in the creation of two National Instruments: National Instrument 21-101, Marketplace Operation, and National Instrument 23- 101, Trading Rules. These instruments provided for an independent regulation services entity to regulate trading on all Canadian marketplaces.

The Investment Industry Regulatory Organization of Canada (IIROC) administers, surveils, and enforces the UMIR, which has been approved as the standard set of trading rules. To ensure compliance with UMIR, IIROC is authorized to monitor real-time trading operations and market-related activities, investigate alleged rule violations, and to administer any settlements and hearings arising from such violations.

UMIR came into effect to ensure integrity and a fair and orderly market following the introduction of the ATSs. More specifically, the rules:

UMIR recognizes that there could be multiple market regulators. In addition, UMIR does not cover every aspect of market regulation. For example, the exchanges still regulate certain trade

- activities that are specific to them, such as setting pre-trade price parameters that will freeze trades

occurring too far away from the last sale price. This particular rule is not covered under UMIR, and consequently, it is not IIROC-enforced.

As already stated, IIROC may be retained as a regulation services provider by traditional exchanges, ATSs, and QTRSs. Alternative trading systems will compete with recognized exchanges by providing trade matching and order execution services. ATSs may not engage in certain exchange typical activities, such as providing listing services to issuers. Quotation and trade reporting systems may operate facilities that are authorized to disseminate price quotations and report completed transactions to the applicable securities commissions.

## The Exchanges

A stock exchange is an organized market in which a participant can trade securities in a publicly visible manner and under recognized guidelines applicable to all registered persons employed by participating organizations' of that exchange.

Ifa marketplace is to be successful, it must be perceived as fair and equitable, where participants

are treated as equals and information is public and visible. It must offer investors a liquid market where:

¢ The spread between the bid and ask on an individual stock is relatively small; ® Trades are frequent; ® Price changes from one trade to the next are not dramatic.

Presently, the exchanges in Canada are the Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX - formerly known as the TSE), the Montreal Exchange (ME), the TSX Venture Exchange (TSX V - formerly CDNX), ICE Futures Canada (formerly the Winnipeg Commodity Exchange) and CNSX. (Note: in Quebec, the Montreal Exchange is called Bourse de Montréal.) IIROC provides real-time monitoring of trading activity on the Canadian equity markets.

The ITROC has the power to govern and regulate the business conduct of its dealer members and their employees and to impose penalties for breaches of the IIROC requirements. The IIROC

also ensures financial and sales compliance of its dealer members. IIROC and the individual exchanges, on the other hand, monitor trading activities and can initiate investigations of potential breaches of UMIR or exchange rules, as well as initiate disciplinary proceedings against participants and access persons as applicable.

In November 1999, the landscape of Canadian exchanges experienced a dramatic change when

the exchanges reached a restructuring agreement resulting in the creation of three distinct, streamlined exchanges:

\* The TSX took over all trading of senior Canadian equities, including all senior equities previously traded on Bourse de Montréal.

¢ The Alberta Stock Exchange and the Vancouver Stock Exchange (VSE) consolidated to form TSX V (formerly Canadian Venture Exchange), which trades all junior equities, including

\* With the TSX's demutualization in 2000, it now has participating organizations rather than members. To comply

with UMIR terminology, we will use the term "participant" to refer to dealers with access to all marketplaces interchangeably with the terms "firm" or "dealer."

## Canadian Exchanges

Canadian securities legislation in most jurisdictions does not specifically define the term "exchange". However, the legislation recognizes a marketplace as an exchange if: ¢ It provides a listing function; \* Prices are set competitively through a bidding process; e All transactions are publicly visible;

e There are rules and regulations governing the conduct of market participants;

¢ Market participants can be disciplined and fines can be levied against those violating the set rules and regulations.

Exchanges are also referred to as auction markets. An auction marketplace can be a centrally

located trading floor or can exist as a network of terminals connected to one computer system that serves as the central location.

A floor-trading environment is based on an open outcry system where traders announce

their wares within the trading square and compete openly for trades by bettering the publicly proclaimed prices. Trades take place face to face between two traders and result in an agreement or contract between two parties. The trader has a great deal of control over an order and can use a certain amount of personal judgement in its execution, within the confines of the client's instructions.

An electronic trading environment offers market participants that are accessing the exchange greater speed and accuracy in trading. In addition, an electronic exchange does not require a centrally located trading floor since it has been replaced by a computer network. The TSX, TSX V, and CNSX are completely electronic trading environments. An example of a combined electronic and open outcry exchange is the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), though hybrid technology has led to increased automation and fewer trading floor staff.

NEGOTIATED MARKET (OVER-THE-COUNTER MARKET) A negotiated marketplace does not follow auction market rules. In a negotiated market, the prices posted are those of a trader, not an investor. There may be another dealer with a client order with a better price, but it will not necessarily be posted.

In a negotiated marketplace, trades are not subject to interference by competing better-priced orders. In addition, negotiated markets do not have a central trading location or exchange through which the trades flow. Trades may be reported to an organization as a matter of record, but that organization has little to do with the mechanics of the trade, which is arranged or negotiated between the two parties. The bond market is an example of a dealer or negotiated market, also called an over-the-counter market.

A trader's duty, first and foremost, must be to the client and the client's order in hand. The client pays a fee for service and it is up to the trader to ensure that each and every order is handled fairly and equitably while not unduly affecting the marketplace. The client's trust in the trader and in the dealer is paramount, and the trader must ensure that the dealer's reputation and goodwill

is upheld. A trader who ignores the importance of the market's integrity can thwart the good intentions of others and destroy the public's goodwill as well as the credibility of the marketplace. As experienced traders will attest, an individual with questionable ethics and poor skills will rarely build the appropriate relationships with clients or competing traders to be successful in the long run.

A trader's duty extends beyond the rules and regulations imposed by IIROC and the exchanges or other regulatory authorities. A trader must go beyond doing what is permitted while avoiding what is prohibited. All market participants are required to follow not only the letter, but also the spirit of the law. This duty extends to the interpretation of all rules and regulations and allows the trader to make judgement calls. Such interpretations must be in the best interest of the client rather than that of the dealer or the trader. A trader must be a trustworthy individual of the highest morals to ensure that clients are treated judiciously.

A trader must also learn from mistakes and errors. Experienced traders know how to solve particular problems. The novice and the experienced trader both depend on associates and supervisors to counsel and advise them when necessary.

Trading errors and mistakes are a common occurrence in any trading organization. These course and reference materials address the problem of unintentional mistakes and errors. Intentional disregard for the rules cannot be addressed by training manuals or training in general. If disregard for the rules and regulations is intentional, such behaviour will be dealt with severely by the market regulators, securities commissions, exchange and dealer, and possibly through the judicial

\| system. Intentional disregard, whether fuelled by greed, a desire to succeed at any cost, or any

/ other reasons, cannot be tolerated in a public marketplace. IIROC and the exchanges will, when faced with intentional disregard for UMIR or exchange requirements, react to the fullest extent of their powers to censure the groups or individuals involved.

Finally, a good trader is not only honest, but also skilled in finding the best market and performing the best execution for a particular trade. A trader may decide that one market has an advantage over another in either price or execution. And although price may be the defining factor for most trades, there will, on occasion, be a need to deal in more than one marketplace to ensure the clean and quick execution of a trade. The ability to make decisions like these to get the best possible execution for the client is what distinguishes a good trader from a mediocre one.

For instance, to ensure the client gets the best price possible, a trade may have to be routed to

a foreign exchange such as the NYSE, the London Stock Exchange, or the NASDAQ. But to execute on these markets, the net price must be better than the local market net price (including currency conversion costs and commissions).

In December 2001, the Canadian Securities Administrators (CSA) adopted National Instrument 21-101, Marketplace Operation, and National Instrument 23-101, Trading Rules, as part of an on-going initiative to create a framework for the competitive operation of traditional exchanges and alternative trading systems (ATSs). At the same time, the Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX) and TSX Venture Exchange (TSX V) initiated a review to determine if the CSA's Trading Rules were appropriate for the structure of their respective markets. A secondary goal of this review was

to determine the extent to which discrepancies and regulatory overlap between the exchanges' rules and the rules adopted by the CSA could be removed and harmonized. This second initiative resulted in the formulation of the Universal Market Integrity Rules (UMIR), which promotes

a fair and orderly market and applies to equity trading on all Canadian marketplaces, including traditional exchanges and ATSs

To meet these objectives, the new harmonized rules were expected to be implemented universally and to be applicable to all marketplaces and everyone accessing them. Given these expectations, the only practicable solution was to design a single set of integrity rules that would promote healthy competition in a controlled environment, but not at the expense of turning market regulation into a tradeable commodity.

Under the original ATS proposal, the responsibility for administering and enforcing the CSA's Trading Rules was supposed to be undertaken by so-called approved agents. Any Canadian exchange could have been an approved agent. Therefore, a number of industry commentators voiced their concerns about conflicts of interest if an ATS was required to be regulated by an exchange with which the ATS competed for order flow. Instead, the responsibility for market regulation for all marketplaces was consolidated into an independent, self-regulatory organization that acts solely as a regulation services provider.

IIROC, which was formed in 2008 by the merger of the Investment Dealers Association (IDA) and Market Regulation Services Inc. is the only regulation services provider in Canada with the mandate to regulate, monitor, administer and police equity trading on all Canadian marketplaces. Although the TSX, TSX V, and CNSX, retain ITROC as their regulation services provider, they have also maintained certain rules and regulations that are specific to their markets, including provisions related to systems operation, market quality, and market structure.

## Agency

An agency transaction occurs when a dealer, through an investment advisor! and on behalf of a client, purchases a security from or sells a security to another party. The dealer receives

a commission or fee from its client for this service. At no time does the dealer itself own the security; rather, it directs the trade on behalf of the client to the trading environment.

In a typical transaction, the client authorizes the dealer to purchase or sell a security on his or her behalf. The client gives explicit instructions to the investment advisor on how the order to buy or to sell is to be executed. The investment advisor arranges or acts as agent to complete the deal with a third party.

This is the standard procedure for a trade involving an exchange-listed stock. In contrast, where the transaction involves a security trading over-the-counter, the dealer usually acts as principal. --™

An order execution account is defined as the account of a client held with a dealer for which the dealer is exempted, in whole or in part, from making a determination on the suitability of a particular trade.

## Principal

A principal transaction occurs when a securities dealer buys and sells securities for its own account, or buys from or sells to a client from its own inventories. Bond trades are often principal trades.

When an over-the-counter transaction occurs and a client's buy order is filled from the dealer's own inventory, the client is dealing only with the dealer, which is the holder of the security. In most cases, the dealer profits from the spread between the purchase price it paid for the security and the price at which the security was sold to the client. Of course, this could also be a loss.

An exchange-traded stock can also involve a principal transaction, with the trader filling the order from inventory to expedite the trade, or with the order being automatically matched (crossed) with an offsetting order at the same price for an inventory account. In these circumstances, although a trade will be marked as principal on the contract, a commission will be charged (the same as what would have been charged on a similar agency trade). A commission is almost always charged to retail clients trading equities.

The Canadian exchanges, which are auction markets, allow both agency and principal trades, although the latter are restricted by the requirement to give priority to client orders in certain circumstances, such as order execution accounts.

In April 2000, the CSA ruled that discount brokers who do not provide investment advice could submit applications for exemption from suitability requirements if certain conditions are met

first. A year later, the IIROC Board approved a proposal to extend this relief to full-service brokers on a trade-by-trade basis. Since then, the ITROC suitability relief proposals have been approved by the CSA. However, limiting relief from suitability obligations to recommended trades is not automatic. To take advantage of this relief, dealers must first submit their applications to the IIROC, in addition to satisfying record-keeping and supervision requirements indicated in IIROC Rule 3200.

## The Order Trader

Each exchange has several categories of traders. Each category of traders has specific duties and regulations to follow, in addition to the general rules of conduct of the particular exchange. There

are different terms used by the exchanges to describe their particular categories of traders. In this manual, the term order trader, or more simply trader, will be applied to individuals involved with the trading function who are properly approved by the appropriate exchange or exchanges.

The trader is responsible for a dealer's client orders. The trader ensures that instructions given in the order are followed, the order is filled in a timely manner and the fills are reported in a timely manner. For example, a client may enter an open order to sell a stock at a price $1.00 above current market prices. The trader should monitor the price of that stock to ensure that the trade is executed once the price limit is met.

A trader may also provide a dealer and its sales force with necessary information about market conditions by sharing indications of interest from other traders. Such indications may include price levels and size of bids or offers, or information about where to purchase a block of a particular stock. Although a trader is permitted to canvas opportunities with other dealers or clients based on a client's indication of interest, a trader is nonetheless required to safeguard client information that is not pertinent to the client's particular indication of interest. Similarly, a trader can never take advantage of a client's indication of interest to benefit the trader.

Of course, a trader can also execute inventory orders, but only at the behest of the dealer. The trader cannot originate such an order, and would simply be following instructions given to him or her by a superior. The trader may work on the floor of the exchange, off the dealer's premises, or with the dealer's trading desk.

## Market Makers

Some exchanges use a market making system to maintain a fair and orderly market in listed issues. Presently the TSX and CNSX employ market makers.

A participant that is approved by the exchange as a market maker is assigned a stock (or stocks) of responsibility (SORs) based on various criteria, such as size and quality of the market being offered and market making performance on the participant's existing SORs. The TSX and CNSX both reserve the right to determine which of the competing market making dealers will be awarded the responsibility. The dealer then assigns an individual responsible designated trader (RDT) to carry out market making duties on the stock.

The RDTs, who are generally more senior and experienced traders, must follow strict guidelines established by the exchange in trading their SORs. RDTs must also ensure that trading in their own accounts is reasonable and consistent with just and equitable principles of trading. The RDT

is also required to assist others in the execution of their orders with respect to SORs.

The RDT trades using the dealer's capital and carries inventories of his or her SORs to ensure a liquid two-way market. RDTs also receive advantages in exchange for the responsibilities assumed and restrictions imposed on their trading. They are entitled to a smaller margin than that required of the general public for similar positions. RDTs, as a matter of market function, are aware of the order flow of their SORs, which can be an intangible benefit. By studying the trading patterns, of their SORs, RDTs can use this information to decide when and how to participate in the market.

The RDTs must be familiar with the trading patterns of their specific SORs. This knowledge

is ascertained by reviewing the daily trading patterns of the stocks, noting volumes and price changes. The RDT develops a sense of what would be considered normal or average price changes.

Price movements are to be minimized so that trading does not appear erratic.

An example of erratic behaviour might be a $5 jump in the price of a security from one trade to the next. However, erratic behaviour for one stock is not necessarily erratic for another. This is not an exact science by any means. RDTs monitor the trading of their SROs for unusual activity to ensure a fair, liquid market for all market participants. Although not an employee of the exchange, the RDT does have responsibility to the exchange in this regard.

Market Makers are appointed on a wide variety of stocks. The exchange negotiates with the market making dealers to set specific requirements for their SORs, as there being is no standard

list of specific requirements that all market makers must meet. For example, there is no rule stating that all quote spreads must be $0.10. These spreads, as noted above, are negotiated on a stock-by-stock basis. The exchanges regularly monitor and review the Market Makers' trading regularly, to ensure compliance with these limits.

## TSX Market Makers

The TSX categorizes listed securities according to tiers for certain purposes. These tiers are determined quarterly, by the average daily value traded in the preceding 12 months. The two major tier categories are Tier A and Tier B, each of which has respective sub-tiers.

Stocks that are categorized as Tier A are the most active ones, while Tier B includes stocks that, on average, trade less actively. Certain Tier B stocks do not have a market maker assigned to them.

The RDT's trading behaviour should increase liquidity in his or her SORs and encourage trading by other market participants. The RDT must ensure that 70% to 80% of personal trades are of a non-disruptive or stabilizing nature. The RDT's trading must moderate price volatility in addition

to adding liquidity and depth. There must be a continuous two-sided market that is competitive with other markets. The RDT must buy and sell the odd-lot and minimum guaranteed fill (MGF) requirements, as well as the mixed-lot requirements. The RDT must have the highest of ethical standards. The RDT's responsibilities include:

\* Posting a market within a maximum spread; for example, $0.10 between the bid and ask; ¢ Filling odd-lot or broken-lot orders for the stock;

\* Executing minimum guaranteed fill orders at the limits agreed upon with the exchange.

## The Arbitrageur

The arbitrageur (or arbitrage trader or 'arb') buys and sells the same or equivalent securities in different markets at different prices, profiting from small price variances. For example, an arbitrageur may buy BCE in Toronto and simultaneously sell BCE in New York. If the sale price

is more than the buy price after converting to Canadian dollars from US funds, the trade is considered successful.

Arbitrageurs are necessary to keep the market liquid and to minimize price differences. The arbitrageur ensures that there is essentially one price for a stock, regardless of whether it trades on only one exchange or also trades on markets outside Canada. Any price discrepancy of a stock listed on more than one exchange should be due only to currency exchange costs. When prices differ by more than this cost, the arbitrageur would step in to bring the prices in line. Due to the benefits arbitrage traders provide, certain exemptions to the short sale rule are granted to assist in price efficiency.

Traders must be extremely knowledgeable about the various types of orders that may be used to execute trades. The trader must know exactly what is meant by any special handling instructions and whether the instructions, as stated on the order, can be executed on a particular exchange.

A brief description of the different orders and other trading terminology follows as a reference for the trader. Bear in mind that many of these orders can be combined in a variety of ways to create innumerable situations for the trader to handle. Special terms orders may be handled differently by each exchange. The Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX) and TSX Venture (TSX V) provide a separate special terms order book. Special terms orders may be entered in this book, but they will have no standing in the regular board lot market. Although the order itself may be the same, the trader must understand how order may be affected by an exchange's trading system. Because special terms orders can be handled differently, these orders will be reviewed after regular orders.

COMMITTED ORDER This is an offer to buy or sell a specific number of shares of a listed security at a specific price that is entered into the Book and that is open for acceptance.

STABILIZING TRADE Purchases made below, or sales made above, the last differently priced trade.

DESTABILIZING TRADE Purchases made above, or sales made below, the last differently priced trade.

NEUTRAL TRADE This is a transaction that would have been a destabilizing trade, except that the trader was unwinding a previously taken position.

IMBALANCE This describes a specific market condition rather than a type of order. When a number of shares being bought outweighs the number of shares being sold (or vice versa), an imbalance is said to have occurred. To ensure fair pricing in fair and equitable markets, the trading, as well as the order flow, must be strictly monitored. It is an important function of a market eanllviat to ensure that a security's price behaviour is not erratic or abnormal in any way.

GOODTILL CANCELLED ORDER A good till cancelled order (GTC) or, as it is sometimes called, a good through order (GTD), is

All marketplaces maintain a central file of outstanding orders known as the Board Lot Order Book, or simply, the Book. There are many different variations of the Book, depending on which marketplace or which system one is referring to, but the purpose of the Book is the same. It is a central file that contains all outstanding orders awaiting execution in the marketplace.

The Book was originally held and maintained by floor traders who developed it as a convenience that allowed them to circulate from one trading square to another during the trading day. Originally, it was merely a notebook, containing records of all outstanding orders, thus allowing trades to take place despite the floor trader's absence from the specific trading square.

With current technology, maintenance of the Book has become easier and automatic, turning it,

in a manner of speaking, into the trading square itself. Orders coming into the marketplace via electronic connections are matched with orders already in the Book, which is now a centralized computer file. Market orders and tradable limit orders are simply matched and executed while other orders that cannot be immediately matched-either because an order is off the market, it

is pre-opening, or, in some systems, special terms and conditions are attached to an order-are queued in sequence by price and time.

Before computers became mainstream on trading floors, separate books or files were maintained for special terms and odd lot trades. Because these orders required special execution or special terms settlement, they were not entered in the Board Lot Order Book and so were not booked. Booked orders thus attained a special status and were accorded certain priorities.

A booked order must be filled once the market crosses its limit price. In contrast, the restrictions imposed on special terms orders also limited execution, and therefore such orders had no guarantee of a fill, even if the market moved beyond the order's price. Because such special terms

##. Disclosed and Undisclosed Orders

On some marketplaces, an order can be classified as disclosed, partially disclosed or undisclosed. A disclosed order is simply an order that is publicly displayed either in the Book or other books. Most orders are entered as disclosed and this expression is not attached to the order; it is assumed that an order is to be fully disclosed unless the trader is instructed otherwise.

A partially disclosed order (also known as an iceberg order) is one in which part of the order is publicly visible while the remainder is held back from public scrutiny for the time being. The client may not want to disrupt the market and possibly drive up the price of the security in the case of a buy order, or drive down the price of the security in the case of a sell order, before a complete fill is received. By not showing a desire to buy, the trader may be able to execute the order more favourably than if the market was totally aware of the extent of the client's intentions.

An undisclosed order allows the trader to watch the market and step in if the order can be filled quickly without any interference or disruption in the marketplace, that is, without abnormal price impact.

Undisclosed orders are risky and do not receive a fill if the market traded above or below the order limit. Because they are not booked, undisclosed orders have neither time nor price priority.

## Attribution Choices and Undisclosed Volume Orders

In February 2002, the Toronto Stock Exchange approved a number of amendments that resulted in the implementation of attribution choices? for orders (formerly referred to as anonymous orders) and undisclosed volume orders. What used to be known as an anonymous order is now known as an unattributed order, and for clarity purposes, the exchange added the definition of an attributed order.

Under the amendments, an unattributed order is defined as an order that is displayed in the Book without the securities dealer's trading number, while an undisclosed volume order is an order for which the size or portion of the order is not displayed in the Book.

According to the previous TSX and TSX Venture Exchange rules, undisclosed orders had no time priority until they were fully disclosed. However, TSX Rule 4-801, and TSX Venture Exchange Rule C.2.01, the Order Priority Rule, now states that an undisclosed order may have time priority if there is no other disclosed order at that price.

The Order Priority Rule also clarifies that an order will lose time priority if its disclosed volume is increased, in which case it must rank behind all other disclosed orders at that price.

With these amendments, the allocation and best execution rules have also changed; however, they will be discussed in more detail later on in this manual.

It is important for the trader to understand the differences between disclosed and undisclosed orders to understand how such special instructions are affected by the priority given to booked orders.

## Introduction

A typical trading day begins well before the opening bell, as there is seldom time to take care of such things as reconciling the previous day's trading activity, confirming inventory positions, and reviewing open (good till cancelled) orders once the opening is under way. As well, both the order trader and responsible designated trader (RDT) should review different news sources such as newspapers and news wires, for essential information that could affect the execution of orders. Knowing that the price of gold has moved up significantly overseas may influence a trader

or RDT to act more aggressively while executing a client's market order to buy shares of a gold producer. Some of the tasks a trader or RDT has prior to the opening bell are discussed below.

In addition to the news wires, the trader and RDT should also be familiar with the exchanges' - aternal news services or bulletin boards. The exchanges provide regular information regarding  their listed issues that pertains specifically to trading. Such information may include items such as

ex dividend dates, margin rates, short interest reports, and restricted securities.

## Trade Reconciliation

The first task both the trader and RDT must perform is a reconciliation of the previous day's trades and orders. Although technological advances have decreased the chances of errors, these can still occur. For example, the price reported on a trade execution may be different than the actual execution price, or markings may have been noted incorrectly, or an order may have been executed but never reported. In an electronic environment, errors may include account number

or account type errors or omitted special terms instructions. Although these errors have more to do with input than execution, it is nonetheless important to review the previous day's trading for completeness. Although it is possible that such errors could be discovered during the day and corrected at that time, some may escape detection until the next day.

Most dealers have back office departments that have responsibility for reconciling the previous day's trades. Although this function is performed by experienced staff, the assistance of the trader may be required to verify specific trade information.

The reconciliation process for the TSX and TSX V has been partially automated. For example, dealers must submit a list of all trades executed (processed) to CDS Clearing and Depository Services Inc. ("CDS Clearing"). Simultaneously, the relevant exchange must also submit a list of trades as executed by each dealer. CDS then compares the files and creates an exception report detailing all differences for each dealer.

The back office staff, in conjunction with the trading desk, determines which file is correct when reviewing the exception report. If the trade was reported erroneously to the client, the dealer can cancel and amend the trade on its books, and then send the client a corrected trade confirmation.

However, this may not always be practical, as the client may not accept the correction. In this

case, the dealer may elect to honour the trade as reported and absorb any loss through its error account.

In an electronic environment, exchange staff has little to do with the inputting of trade data and, consequently, errors are not the responsibility of the exchange. Assuming that a correct stock symbol and price have been entered, the chances of a pricing error are remote because the system executes the orders.

In addition, professional trading accounts, also known as inventories, must be reviewed daily to ensure that all transactions have been processed correctly. It is possible for a trade to end up in the wrong inventory. The trader or RDT should ensure that the processed trades accurately reflect the previous day's trading. As RDTs are often compensated based on profits generated on the inventory, such an error can have a direct impact on the RDT's income.

There are no specific rules regulating the process of reconciliation by the trader or RDT. However,

the trader or RDT should reconcile trading executed on behalf of the dealer or an inventory. Electronic trading was designed to minimize the possibility of errors.

## Open Orders

Along with the general review of the trades processed, the trader should ensure that all unfilled open orders were processed correctly. To that end, the trader must review open orders and other items carried over from the previous day. All open (good till cancelled) orders entered the previous day are shown in the Book.

All new good till cancelled (GTC) orders entered the previous day must be checked to ensure that they were not inadvertently entered as day orders.

In some electronic environments (but not on the TSX or TSX V), undisclosed orders can be queued but are not booked. This means that the order can be entered into the system, but has no priority and cannot be seen by others in a display of all booked orders. It is, in effect, completely hidden from public scrutiny. The order is in the trader's queue and is visible to the trader only; other traders cannot see or respond to the order. Even in the electronic environment, undisclosed orders have no standing. Thus, GTC orders that are recorded in error as undisclosed orders can be potential liabilities, having no standing in the queue. In some systems, it is possible that a trade through could occur, causing a loss for the dealer.

## Adjusting Ex Dividend Orders

The TSX will decide when a security begins trading on an ex dividend basis. Other marketplaces may not announce or determine the ex dividend date, which means that the settlement date of a trade will determine whether the security is cumulative or ex dividend if the trade settles on or before the issuer-declared record date. In most cases, the ex dividend date is set at two business days before the record date, although this is not always the case. All exchanges publish daily notices in their bulletin system for their participating organizations (POs), advising which

securities are about to begin, or have begun, trading on an ex dividend basis.

The trader should adjust downward the prices of all GTC orders to buy securities that have gone ex dividend, by the amount of the dividend, as this is not done automatically by the exchange. The rationale is that a client wishing to purchase a particular security at $10.00 with an expected dividend of $0.50 would not find the security as attractive if the $0.50 dividend were not going to be received. The order should be reduced by the $0.50 dividend and priced at $9.50.

A client may request, in the same circumstances, that the buy order not be reduced on the ex dividend date. This is known as a do not reduce (DNR) order.

Stop loss orders, in the absence of instructions to the contrary, must be adjusted downward as well. Some dealers may have a policy to advise clients with orders for securities about to trade on an ex dividend basis, and the client decides whether to adjust the order. It is important that the investment advisor be aware of the dealer's ex dividend policy.

Short sales are sales of a security that the seller does not own. Short sale transactions are often speculative in nature, and are entered into by investors who believe the price of the security is about to fall. The seller must borrow the securities to consummate the sale, with the expectation of later buying back the shares at a lower price and returning them to the lender. The difference

Short sellers are not always bearish about a particular stock. In fact, a large percentage of short selling is done to hedge certain long positions. For example, very conservative investors may use short sales of common stocks to hedge long positions in convertible securities. Or, some hedge traders will put on a covered position at a certain net spread with the hopes of unwinding the position later at a profit.

## Introduction

'The minimum guaranteed fill (MGF) is an exchange rule found only on the TSX. It guarantees

the execution of a tradable client order up to a certain number of shares.

Tradable orders are buy orders with limits equal to or higher than the current offering price, or

sell orders with limits equal to or lower than the current bid price, or any market-priced order. Non-client and principal orders, or any orders that are greater than the MGF amount cannot - participate in the MGF and are classified as MGF-ineligible orders.

RDTs are required to execute or fill MGF orders at the best bid or ask price if there is insufficient stock in the Book to fill the order. The TSX system automatically generates these fills when applicable, and the RDT will see the subsequent fill report indicating that there was an MGF-type fill on his or her trading terminal. Because there is an upper limit on the guaranteed fill amount,

it may appear that the minimum guaranteed fill should really read maximum. The minimum, though, refers to the exchange's determination of a minimum number of shares for which the RDT must guarantee a fill. This type of rule assures investors that market and tradable limit orders will be filled expediently. This increases market depth and liquidity and puts an end to incomplete fills (at least up to the MGF). If used properly, the MGF can also encourage more intra-day trading by participants and can increase overall confidence in the market.

The minimum size of the MGF is one share less than two board lots, and the maximum allowed MGEF size is 15 board lots plus the odd lot. For stocks with a board lot size of 100 shares, the minimum MGF is 199 shares (although most stocks have an MGF of at least 299 shares), and the maximum MGEF is 1,599 shares. Although the exchanges use trademark acronyms to describe their MGF services, this manual will simply use the acronym MGF.

A high-volume stock with good liquidity and market depth may have a higher MGF than a stock that is thinly traded. The minimum again refers to an exchange-determined amount. The MGF amount is negotiated between the market making dealer and the exchange. It can be lowered, with the approval of the exchange, if a reasonable case can be made for doing so. The decision to lower the MGF would take into account the risk involved in assuming positions and the ability to unwind a position in a timely manner.

## MGF and Splitting Orders

Orders for a larger number of shares than the MGF for a particular stock cannot be split into several smaller orders and entered into the MGF facility separately, nor should numerous smaller orders for a particular client be repeatedly entered into the MGF facility.

However, there may be many valid reasons for splitting a larger order into two or more smaller orders, or one client repeatedly executing a number of smaller orders. For example, the trader may recognize that a particular security trades equally well on both the TSX and another market and may decide to split an order, entering a portion in each market to obtain a complete fill for the client. Or, a trader may feel that disclosing a large order all at once may not be the best strategy and may decide that entering only part of the order in stages is the best approach. Also, advancing technology has allowed increasing numbers of retail clients to engage in active trading, or "day trading." Whatever the reason for splitting an order, or entering multiple single orders for one client, the split orders should not be entered into the MGF facility.

In order to avoid a violation in the above scenarios, the order must be designated as MGF-

ineligible (referred to as the "BK" marker). When an order is entered as MGF-ineligible, the system is instructed to trade with the Book only. The order will not receive a minimum guaranteed fill even if it meets the criteria. As mentioned earlier, however, if the RDT's participation is active on that side of the market, even those client orders marked MGF-ineligible will be guaranteed a fill, as long as they otherwise meet the MGEF criteria.

Understanding the concepts of minimum guaranteed fills and the participation feature, and knowing how to avoid misusing the systems by using the MGF-ineligible marker, should help the trader avoid most violations. However, there are violations that occur not because the trader was unaware of the rules or did not know how to keep an order from entering the facility; rather, the main cause of MGF violations is simply that the trader was unaware that a larger order had been split into smaller orders. It should be made obvious that an order has been split when it has been entered into two different markets. Likewise, a trader may not enter an order to sell a total of 10,000 shares, but may elect instead to enter only 2,000 shares at a time. The reasoning for these splits is generally related to the timing of the execution, and the trader may be attempting to not unduly influence the market. Such trades have not been split to take advantage of the MGE, but are ineligible for the MGF nonetheless and must be marked MGF-ineligible.

## Introduction

A jitney trade is a trade where a participant acts on behalf of another participant. This includes introducing or carrying broker relationships.

Traders must understand the technical definition of a jitney transaction because there are a number of rules governing how such trades may be executed. Incorrectly identifying a client order as a jitney trade may result in a disadvantage to the client and the dealer, and identifying

In accordance with the requirements of National Instrument 23-101 and UMIR, an order that is entered on a marketplace as a jitney order must contain the identifier of the participant for or on behalf of whom the order is entered. ## Jitney Crosses

Jitney crosses are allowed only at prices between the bid and ask. This is to ensure that the rules governing priority and allocation are not circumvented through the use of a jitney.

## Regulatory Halts and Delays

When a market regulator believes that important information concerning a listed security is about to be made public, the market regulator may instruct the exchange to halt or delay trading of that security for a short time. The halt was designed to ensure sufficient time for all market participants to gain equal access to new material information.

The industry disclosure policies require public companies to issue a press release immediately, or as soon as practicable, after trading in a security is halted. This is to ensure that all market transactions are conducted on a level playing field and to minimize the duration of trading halts.

All related securities, including listed options, are also halted. However, index products such as options, futures, and participation units generally are not halted if their underlying or component stock is halted.

From time to time, securities commissions may issue a cease trading order against a listed or quoted issuer. The reasons may include failing to file financial reports on time or filing misleading

or incomplete information. When a cease trading order is issued, a regulatory halt is imposed.

While a security is halted or delayed for regulatory reasons, no trades may occur on a marketplace

or over the counter in the halted stock. ## Introduction

The electronic nature of the exchanges' trading systems has reduced, but not entirely eliminated, the possibility of market manipulation at the close. Manipulative trading at the close can be accomplished by a number of methods, but in all cases such trading is an attempt by the trader

or the person entering the order to alter the effective and proper closing price of a security. The reasons behind such a move are clear, and usually include:

\* Increasing the price of the security to secure a more favourable loan value for any long positions or a more favourable margin position for any short positions;

\* Causing an inventory to be overvalued to hide losses or increase the pay-out to the trader;

\* Giving the impression of more activity in a particular security than is really there to elicit further trades; \* Enhancing the performance of a portfolio or fund.

## Market-on-Close Orders

Market-at-the-close orders, also referred to as market-on-close (MOC) orders, are orders that are intended to be executed at the close of business, regardless of price (within reason). Years ago, it was easy for traders with MOC orders to work together to ensure participation at the end of the

day. With the advent of electronic trading, it is no longer possible to guarantee that a particular trade will be the last one of the day. It may be possible for an order to arrive electronically or otherwise after MOC orders have been entered and filled. If a trader has multiple MOC orders to

execute, it is physically impossible to trade all of the orders at one second prior to the close. Even

if it were possible, another order could come in and set a new closing price a few seconds before the close. Because of these inherent difficulties, traders usually handle MOC on a best efforts basis.

MOC orders are used by traders and clients for a number of reasons. Some clients may feel that, on average, the price for a particular stock is most attractive at the end of the day. Others may use MOC orders as part of a strategy to obtain an average price over a period of time. Still others may be entering into certain derivative or hedge trading strategies that are tied into closing index levels. Whatever the reason, it is vital that MOC orders do not cause undue volatility in the marketplace. A large MOC order could cause large price changes in a security, as there is no time for other market participants to react to such orders. Even a relatively small MOC order could cause an aberration in the price of a thinly traded security.

Because of this concern, MOC orders must be handled according to the following guidelines:

° Traders executing MOC orders must adhere to the high-closing rules, which state that the closing price must be reasonable for a particular security.

If, due to market depth, a MOC order will move the market in a security, the move must be done in an orderly fashion. This means that other market participants must have time

to react to the changing market. The more a market may be moved by an MOC order, the ie longer the time period to react.

¢ MOC orders may be exempt from the restrictions on short selling and may be executed at a price below the last sale price of a security, but must be marked as short exempt.

CLOSING PRICE ORDERS National Instrument 21-101, Marketplace Operation, requires that each marketplace establish its own operating hours. The Instrument does not require each marketplace to adopt the traditional operating hours of the current exchanges in Canada. To facilitate trading at the closing price, trades may be permitted in special facilities at the closing price.

The ability to execute trades at the last sale price of a trading session accommodates index rebalancing at the closing price. For example, the value of the S&P/TSX 60 Index is calculated based on the prices of the constituent securities on the Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX); it does not take into account the prices of trades on other marketplaces even when those other marketplaces may be open earlier or later than the regular trading session on the TSX. Therefore, various exchange-traded funds, mutual funds, and other financial instruments that are designed to track certain indices need to execute trades in the index's constituent securities at the closing price used to calculate the underlying index.

On the TSX, the closing price of any security that is a constituent of an index (or any security that is being added to an index) is determined in the MOC facility through the entry of a market- on-close order. In addition, once the closing price has been determined on the TSX through the MOC facility, trading occurs at the closing price during the last sale trading session from 4:15 to 5:00 p.m.

To accommodate such trading, recent amendments to the Universal Market Integrity Rules (UMIR) include a provision for a closing price order, which is defined as a trade that takes place

at the closing price of the security in a trade on the marketplace on that trading day and that is executed subsequent to the establishment of the closing price. ## introduction

It is not surprising that program trading is very often confused with basket trading. In fact, many traders use the two terms interchangeably. A program trade is generally considered to be the simultaneous execution of a series of orders for the securities that make up an underlying index along with the appropriate hedge. On the other hand, a basket trade is the purchase or sale of a group of securities as one transaction.

INDEX ARBITRAGE One of the more common forms of basket trading is index arbitrage trading between interrelated securities, such as S&P/TSX 60 (SXF) futures and the underlying basket of stocks that comprise Most of the time, the relative markets of the above securities will trade in line with each other, since they are driven by the prices of the underlying basket of 60 stocks. From time to time certain anomalies may arise. A speculator who is bullish about the direction of the market may decide

to buy an SXF contract in the hopes of profiting from the rising Index. The speculator may be willing to pay a slight premium to ensure that the buy order will be filled, and therefore enters a bid for the futures contract that is higher than the existing best bid.

An arbitrageur may see this opportunity to sell the futures contract to the speculator at a premium

bid, and simultaneously buy whichever of the other four instruments is currently the cheapest hedge. The hedge may be a purchase of an appropriate quantity of S&P/TSX 60 Index (SXO) options, or an appropriate quantity of iUnits, or the appropriate quantity of each of the 60 underlying stocks. At this point, the speculator is happy because the futures contract has been purchased, and the arbitrageur is happy because a small profit has been generated by the price differential between the two instruments.

Of course, the profit calculations for the arbitrageur would have to include any costs associated with carrying the position, which in the case of purchasing shares of each of the 60 stocks in

the basket may be significant. However, if the speculator's bid was not attractive enough, the arbitrageur would simply not participate.

Once the arbitrageur has put on the hedge position, it is simply a matter of waiting until one of the instruments expires, which is usually on the third Friday of each month, or the position is closed out. The arbitrageur will have to ensure that both sides of the hedge position expire on the same day or risk being exposed if only one side is closed out. If the arbitrageur is lucky, another investor may come in to the market willing to trade in one of the instruments, in which case the positions would be unwound, again at an attractive price. Typically though, an arbitrageur will buy and sell various instruments throughout the month as different opportunities present themselves, with the only set goal of remaining hedged at all times.

## Formal Take-over Bids

A formal take-over bid is an offer to acquire 20% or more of the outstanding voting or equity securities of a target company. The definition includes not only an offer to purchase, but also an acceptance of an offer to sell or a combination of the two. Under the provincial securities acts, the 20% threshold must include all current holdings of voting, equity, or unissued securities owned by the offeror and its joined actors. As well, the offeror and its joint actors are deemed to own all securities that could be obtained by the exercise of any rights, warrants, or convertible securities held.

As mentioned above, take-over bids are regulated by provincial securities acts, applicable marketplaces, and, to a very limited extent, UMIR. Rules and regulations governing take-over bids have been established to ensure these transactions are fair to everyone involved; that is, shareholders, target companies, and offerors. For example, take-over bid rules are governed by

coattail rules that eliminate discrimination against minority or non-voting shareholders. This means that even though take-over bids are made to voting shareholders only, coattail provisions allow non-voting shareholders to vote on take-over bids just like voting shareholders.

## Normal Course Purchases

Normal course purchases are exempt from the formal take-over bid rules. A normal course purchase is any purchase or series of purchases that would otherwise be considered a take-over bid (that is, the take-over bid threshold is crossed), but where the total number of shares being purchased by the offeror (buyer) will not exceed 5% of the listed voting or equity securities of

a company in any 12-month period. If a buyer wants to make purchases that will exceed this take-over bid threshold, or if the buyer has already crossed the threshold, then purchases may not be made under normal course purchase rules. A formal take-over bid must be announced and executed in accordance with the appropriate securities acts and marketplace rules.

The 12-month period under which normal course purchases may be made is called a rolling period. That is, ifa shareholder purchases 2% on June 1 of year one, and another 3% on September 15 of year one, then the shareholder would not be able to make any more purchases until June 2 of year two, at which time an additional 2% could be purchased. Assuming this additional purchase of 2% was made, the buyer would then have to wait until September 16 of year two, at which time another 3% could be bought, and so on.

## issuer Bids

Issuer bids are bids or purchases by a company for its own securities. A company may want to purchase its own securities for a number of reasons, including the following:

\* The issuer may believe that its securities are undervalued, or feels that its securities are an

It is not uncommon for a trader to receive a request from a client, via a salesperson, to size a bid or offer for a large block of stock. The trader would approach other traders who had indicated that they were bidders or sellers, and would ask how much of the stock they were willing to buy or

A trader is required to protect the confidentiality of a client order if the client order has not been exposed to a marketplace. a particular security (based on the historical liquidity of that security), a trader is permitted to canvass opportunities with other dealers or clients to facilitate the trade before the client order

is exposed to a marketplace. Although this type of activity is permitted, a trader is nonetheless required to safeguard client information that is not pertinent to that specific client order.

Traders always have the responsibility to make reasonable efforts to obtain the best possible price for client orders. Under UMIR 5.2, a trader has an obligation to make reasonable efforts

to fill better-priced orders on a marketplace before executing a trade at an inferior price on another marketplace or a foreign market. The fundamental principle of all recognized marketplaces is that they are true auction markets-the buyer with the highest bid will have priority over all other bidders and the seller with the lowest ask will have priority over all other sellers. If a trader executes a trade on a marketplace at a price that is inferior to another marketplace's price, then this principle fails.

This applies even if the client directs the trade to a marketplace posting the inferior price. It would seem unlikely that a client would do so, but perhaps he or she wanted to buy a large block of stock and saw a large offering on that exchange, at a price slightly higher than is presently available - - on another exchange. the better price on the other exchange. Even if the client chooses to ignore the small, better-priced offering in favour of purchasing the large block, the trader still has an obligation to purchase the a small offering to preserve the auction process. A client may also prefer that all orders are executed solely on one marketplace, but the trader's obligation to take out better-priced orders still exists.

## Responsibility for Supervision and Compliance

Participants are responsible for supervising their employees, directors, officers, and, if applicable, partners, to ensure that trading in securities on a marketplace (an exchange, quotation and trade reporting system (QTRS), or alternative trading system) is carried out in compliance with the applicable provisions of securities legislation, Universal Market Integrity Rules (UMIR), and the trading and marketplace rules of individual exchanges or QTRSs (together, the "requirements"). Participants are required to develop and implement a clearly defined set of internal policies and procedures that are reasonably designed to prevent and detect violations of the requirements.

The participant's board of directors is responsible for the overall stewardship of the dealer, with

a specific responsibility to supervise the management of the dealer. Directors must ensure that major risks of non-compliance have been identified and that appropriate risk management policies and procedures have been implemented.

The responsibility for trading supervision further filters down to the participant's management team, which must ensure that the supervision system adopted by the participant is effectively carried out. Usually, the supervisory duty is delegated to the head of trading, who is responsible for competent and thorough supervision of the participant's trading personnel and their trading activities. i ee

A market regulator retained by a particular marketplace is responsible for reviewing the supervision system employed by a participant who is accessing that marketplace. The market regulator must consider whether the system was designed reasonably well to prevent and detect violations of applicable rules and regulations and whether the system was followed.

Finally, the participant's compliance department is responsible for monitoring and reporting adherence to rules, regulations, requirements, policies, and procedures. In doing so, the compliance department must develop and implement a reasonably designed monitoring system. The results of its reviews must be reported back to the participant's management team and, where

\| necessary, to the board of directors or its equivalent. In turn, the board and management must ensure that the compliance department is adequately funded, staffed, and empowered to meets its responsibilities.

However, IIROC also does not relieve participants from complying with any specific requirements

that may apply. Consequently, participants are required to develop and implement supervision and compliance procedures that are not only appropriate for their lines of business, but also meet,

## Artificial Pricing

One of the more difficult situations that can arise in trading occurs when an order is placed late in the day, or earlier in the day but accompanied by instructions that require the order to be executed

at or near the close. This type of order will have the effect of significantly changing the bid, ask, or

last sale of a stock. In fact, UMIR and the rules of the marketplaces strictly prohibit participants and access persons from making a bid or offer, or from executing any trade, if it would establish

an artificial price or quotation, or set a high or low closing price for the stock. Participants must

ensure that trading staff are aware of the possibility of such orders, particularly at the end of the month, quarter, and year. If a trader suspects that the purpose of an order may be to set an

artificial price or a high or low closing price, he or she should question the client and bring the matter to the attention of the participant's compliance area and the trading supervisor.

## Frontrunning

No one is allowed to take advantage of a client's order by trading ahead of it. This includes traders of both the dealer accepting the order and any trader of a competing dealer with advance knowledge of the order. This applies to trades in the same security or a related security such as

a convertible or option. Trading ahead of, and to the disadvantage of, a client order is known

as frontrunning, and is a serious offence. However, trading on non-specific information such as rumours does not constitute frontrunning.

Trading in the marketplace based on material, non-public information about the affairs of the underlying company constitutes insider trading, and is prohibited by law. Knowledge of a large order about to enter the marketplace may not be considered material information about the affairs of the company; however, large orders may have an impact on the market price of the security. For example, an order to sell a large block of stock would likely move the stock price lower. It would be very easy for a person, knowing that such a large order to sell a block of stock is about to be entered, to sell stock short for their own account and buy it back at a lower price once the other order is completed. Another means of profiting illegally could occur by purchasing put options on the security, again prior to the execution of the client's large sell order. The rules prohibiting frontrunning also include trading in other markets, including derivatives markets such as futures; a and options.

## Beneficial Ownership

The rules regarding manipulative or deceptive practices also deal with the concept of change of beneficial ownership. Historically, TSX Venture Exchange required trades between spouses to be executed off-marketplace. The TSX, on the other hand, viewed such trades as actual changes in beneficial ownership and required them to be executed on a marketplace. With UMIR coming into force on April 1, 2002, ROC requires that trades involving a change in beneficial and economic ownership be traded on a marketplace on which the participant is a participating organization, user, or subscriber.

To enforce this, Rule 6.4 of UMIR, Trades To Be on a Marketplace, requires that all trades be executed on a marketplace where a participant is acting as a principal or agent, unless the trade

falls within one of the exemptions to the rule. Trades that do not constitute either a change in beneficial or economic ownership must not take place on a marketplace as they are, in fact, manipulative and deceptive methods of trading as prescribed under Rule 2.2 of UMIR. For example, a transfer of stock between an individual and a wholly owned corporation involves a change of beneficial ownership, but it does not constitute a change in economic ownership and must therefore be executed off-marketplace.

Every RRSP season, securities dealers receive numerous requests to execute off-marketplace transactions between spouses for income tax or RRSP purposes.

As a result, IIROC has indicated that trades for tax purposes may be done off-marketplace and without prior consultation with IIROC provided that the trade:

% Does not constitute a legal or beneficial change in ownership of a security; '© Is not intended to evade tax or securities laws, contravene UMIR, or contravene the rules and regulations of the applicable marketplace; \* Is executed at a price that is within the context of the market for that particular security at the time of the trade.

This section of the manual is intended to provide an understanding of alternative trading systems (ATSs), as they are becoming increasingly common in Canadian capital markets.

# BACKGROUND -\_

Competition for the trading of equities is not new in Canada; prior to 1999, there were five Canadian stock exchanges with many interlisted securities. The primary markets for senior securities were Montreal and Toronto, while junior listings were shared by the Alberta and Vancouver Stock Exchanges. The decisions a trader would make in how an order was executed would typically be based on the cost of executing on a particular exchange, as well as the available liquidity, trading methodology, and technology.

Between 1999 and 2002, the Canadian equity exchanges consolidated and moved toward becoming profit-motivated companies, rather than the not-for-profit associations that they had

\| been when owned and controlled by dealers. This competitive environment has led to the creation of numerous ATSs in Canada. Access to these systems is offered to established dealers, and in some cases to investing institutions, allowing these institutions to bypass the traditional structure.

ATSs, or electronic communications networks as they are sometimes called, originated with the introduction of the U.S. Securities Act Amendments of 1975, which removed the requirement ee for all trading in listed securities to take place on an exchange. "Reg ATS", an SEC regulation introduced in the United States in the late 1990s, and changes to securities regulation in Canada (NI 21-101 and NI 23-101) have a provided a framework for the introduction and operation of a variety of marketplaces. All of the ATSs in Canada are regulated by IIROC.

The Toronto Stock Exchange (TSX) offers a completely transparent book. Traders and the public alike can see the multiple bid and ask prices, order sizes, and full post-trade disclosure, thereby seeing what dealers have bought and sold and in what quantities and prices.

Other marketplaces will provide aggregate sizes and price levels of orders in the Board Lot Book and a posting of all trades, but will keep the trading participants anonymous. There has been

a movement by some exchanges, such as the Australian Securities Exchange, to go from a fully transparent book such as the TSX's to a multiple-price-level model. One key difference is that, with the Australian Securities Exchange's model, dealer identification is made available three days after the trade date.

Some ATSs offer a completely "dark book", with no bid or ask information provided and post- trade information limited to quantity and price.

Traditionally, pools of liquidity have been associated with exchanges, or with Market Makers

in the case of over-the-counter markets (OTC). Current thought, however, is that the key to executing large block transactions is in discovering and unlocking liquidity that also includes an institution's large position, or its desire to take on a large position. This is key to understanding the value that ATSs can add to the marketplace.

Increasingly, institutions have become focused on their transaction cost analysis, which includes

all of the costs of executing a transaction. The transaction, settlement, and custodial fees are reasonably easy to determine. The cost of execution, however, can be much more difficult to determine because of slippage, or the price impact that a large order has on the marketplace. Typically, slippage is attributed to leakage, or the market gaining knowledge of a pending or current large order. To prevent these issues, ATSs are created as alternatives to conventional trading with unique trading strategies.

¢ Order Driven Market. These are auction markets where orders are grouped until they reach a certain amount, and then executed together at a predetermined time. \* - Crossing Network. BEST PRICE OBLIGATION Although the best price obligation is part of the best execution obligation, it is primarily viewed as

a market integrity issue. Dealers have an obligation to satisfy all displayed better bids and offers in all marketplaces before executing a trade at a price outside those bids and offers.

# G°9 Scale Order

An order to buy or sell two or more lots of the same security at designated price intervals. If the first part of a scale order is a limit, then

all other limits are set when the scale order is entered. If the first part of the scale order is

a market order, the limits are then computed from the price at which the first order was

A person holding separately, or in combination with other persons, 20% or more of the outstanding voting securities of an issuer. (UMIR) Short Position A position sold before it is purchased. Short Sale Order An order for the sale of a security which the

seller does not own.
