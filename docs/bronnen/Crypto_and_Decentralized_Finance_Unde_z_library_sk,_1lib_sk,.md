# Introduction

Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) 1 has increasingly been discussed as a foundational infrastructure for the digital transformation of ﬁnancial services, with both potential beneﬁts and challenges for market organization and risk management (Leo and Panetta, 2023; CPMI, 2017). In this setting, decentralized ﬁnance (DeFi) has emerged as a rapidly evolving ecosystem of crypto-asset services enabled by smart contracts 2 deployed on blockchains and protocol-based coordination. The DeFi paradigm allows for the execution of trading, lending, payments, and other ﬁnancial services without the need for traditional intermediaries. It thereby replicates several core services of traditional ﬁnance (TradFi) while reconﬁguring the way ﬁnancial coordination, accountability, and enforcement are achieved (Aramonte et al., 2021; FSB, 2024). This new paradigm has attracted a growing community of both users and developers, and it has also generated new forms of fragility. In contrast to institution-centered ﬁnance, where governance, risk controls, and legal accountability are anchored in regulated entities, DeFi relies on protocol design choices and technical infrastructures that operationalize trust among participants. Therefore, vulnerabilities may originate from market misconduct and structural weaknesses in the underlying infrastructure, such as deﬁciencies in software implementation, ﬂawed incentive mechanisms, and inter-protocol dependencies.

The rise of decentralized ﬁnance (DeFi) has coincided with pronounced volatility and recurrent boom-and-bust cycles in crypto-asset markets (Aramonte et al., 2021; IMF, 2022). Periods of rapid expansion have been followed by sharp contractions, often triggered by conﬁdence shocks, governance failures, technical vulnerabilities, or the destabilization of key crypto-assets. # Chapter 1 **Financial Intermediation and Trust ** **in Deﬁ **

**Abstract ** The rise of decentralized ﬁnance compels a reconsideration of the very foundations of ﬁnancial intermediation. In conventional systems, trust is institutionalized-anchored in regulated entities, contractual enforceability, and supervisory oversight. DeFi, by contrast, embeds trust within code, governance arrangements, and infrastructural components such as oracles and cross-chain bridges. This relocation does not signify the disappearance of intermediation; rather, it signals its redistribution across technical and organizational layers. The chapter argues that this structural reconﬁguration reshapes accountability and generates novel dependencies, which in turn condition the speciﬁc vulnerabilities examined in subsequent chapters.

## 1.1

**Financial Intermediation and Trust: From Institutions ** **to Protocols **

Traditional ﬁnance organises ﬁnancial intermediation around legally recognised institutions (i.e., banks, insurance companies, brokers, exchanges and others) framing intermediation as an institutionalised process embedded within a system of rights, duties, and enforceable accountability. Within this institutional framework, intermediaries perform core ﬁnancial functions, including the screening and monitoring of borrowers, the custody of assets, the provision of liquidity through market-making activities, the execution and settlement of transactions, the allocation of capital, and the transfer and pooling of risk. These functions are all carried out within a formal governance architecture that subjects institutions to prudential supervision, capital and liquidity requirements, internal control mechanisms, compliance obligations, and conduct-of-business standards. This layered regulatory environment is designed to mitigate information asymmetries and agency conﬂicts, align incentives between principals and agents, and contain systemic externalities internalising risks that would otherwise be dispersed across market participants or the broader ﬁnancial system.

This prevailing institutional architecture began to be challenged by the emergence of DLT technologies, and in particular by blockchain architectures that demonstrated how transaction ordering and settlement could be achieved in open networks

through cryptographic veriﬁcation and consensus 1 rather than through a single trusted operator. The advent of Bitcoin has introduced a new peer-to-peer network design aimed at reducing reliance on ﬁnancial intermediaries for transaction validation and preventing double spending, i.e., the reuse of the same digital unit in multiple transactions. Building on this innovation, the idea of smart contracts, ﬁrst conceptualised as "computerised transaction protocols" that can execute contractual clauses, provided a conceptual bridge from digital payments to programmable ﬁnancial logic (Szabo, 1996 ). The launch of general-purpose smart contract platforms, most notably Ethereum, made it possible to encode more complex ﬁnancial functions directly on-chain, enabling applications of automated exchange, collateralised lending, and protocol interactions. Institutional analyses have since discussed DLTs as a potential redesign of payment, clearing, and settlement arrangements highlighting both opportunities and new risk and governance questions when key functions are shifted from organisations to shared infrastructures (CPMI, 2017 ).

DeFi reconﬁgures this architecture by shifting parts of intermediation from institutions to protocols. In other words, instead of relying on organisational hierarchies and regulatory supervision, DeFi relies on (i) smart contracts that encode rules for transactions and state updates; (ii) crypto-economic incentives that coordinate behaviour; and (iii) modular infrastructures (e.g., oracles, bridges, custody solutions, frontends) that connect protocol logic to market reality. Therefore, "disintermediation" is often better understood as "re-intermediation". In fact, intermediating functions do not disappear, they are decomposed and redistributed across code, governance, and infrastructure providers (Castillo León & Lehar, 2026 ). For instance, market making is implemented through Automated Market Maker (AMM) designs, credit allocation is frequently implemented through collateral rules and liquidation mechanisms, and settlement is anchored in blockchain consensus rather than institutional clearing and settlement systems. This shift deeply affects the notion of accountability. For instance, in TradFi, failures can be addressed through ex post enforcement against identiﬁable entities, through supervision, contractual remedies, or liability regimes. In DeFi, failures may be harder to remediate because the relevant "decision makers" can be dispersed (DAO governance), pseudonymous, or external to the protocol (e.g., oracle operators or bridge validators). Therefore, to understand DeFi, it is essential to characterise how intermediation is structured and where its critical dependencies lie.

## 1.2 **The Reconﬁguration of Trust in Decentralized Finance **

Trust in ﬁnancial systems generally concerns the reliability of counterparties, the integrity of transaction execution, the stability of infrastructures, and the enforceability of rules. In institution-based systems, trust is supported by legal frameworks, supervision, reputational constraints, and institutional risk management. In DeFi, by contrast, trust is reconﬁgured along different dimensions (IOSCO, 2023 ). First, trust shifts from institutional discretion to rule-based execution. Smart contracts can enable predictable execution. Thus, if correctly speciﬁed and implemented, they reduce certain forms of discretionary behaviour and operational opacity since the source code is accessible and veriﬁable from any of the contractors. This trust in code, however, is conditional (Atzei et al., 2017 ). It presumes that the code is correct, that assumptions are valid under stress, and that the supporting infrastructure (e.g., blockchain security, oracle feeds) behaves as expected. Code-based execution can therefore replace some agency problems but introduce new ones, such as software vulnerabilities, design ﬂaws, and correlated failure modes.

Additionally, trust becomes sensitive to governance and upgradeability. Many protocols evolve through upgrades, parameter changes, and governance decisions. This means that users must trust the current code and the processes that can change with it. Who participates in the consensus mechanism, who votes, how quickly vulnerabilities are addressed, and whether emergency powers exist (and how they are protected) are all factors to take into account. Even in systems labelled "decentralized," governance power can be concentrated through token holdings, delegation structures, or privileged roles, 2 creating forms of "hidden centralization" that matter for both resilience and abuse. Furthermore, trust depends on boundary infrastructures that connect protocols to the external environment. For instance, among others, oracles 3 translate market information into on-chain state, bridges connect liquidity and assets across chains, front ends mediate user access, custody tools and wallets secure private keys, and privacy infrastructures affect traceability and enforcement. These components are often less visible than the core protocol but can become critical points of failure or exploitation. Importantly, users may not clearly distinguish between protocol risk and infrastructure risk, even though the operational consequences can be similar (Zetzsche et al., 2020 ). Finally, trust is

## 1.3 **Protocol-Based Intermediation as Organisational Logic **

The transition from institution-based to protocol-based intermediation reconﬁgures the underlying architecture of the ﬁnancial system. In traditional ﬁnance, the operation of markets relies on organisational routines, discretionary judgement, and supervisory oversight, with legal accountability functioning as an ex-post mechanism of enforcement and risk containment. The institutional form is therefore central to the production and enforcement of ﬁnancial rules. By contrast, decentralised ﬁnance restructures intermediation around software-based procedures embedded in executable code. Participants interact through smart contracts deployed on shared ledgers, where transaction rules are predeﬁned and automatically enforced by protocol logic rather than by managerial discretion or regulatory intervention. What would traditionally be understood as a ﬁnancial institution is decomposed into a layered technological conﬁguration in which protocol design, governance mechanisms, and ancillary service interfaces collectively perform exchange, credit, custody, and settlement functions (Werbach, 2018 ). In this setting, institutional authority is partially displaced by computational execution, and coordination emerges from interoperable modules rather than from vertically integrated organisational entities.

A deﬁning trait of this model is its preference for ex-ante speciﬁcation over ex-post discretion. Key conditions (e.g. collateral thresholds, liquidation triggers, fee rules, or priority logic) are written into contracts and executed automatically once the relevant state is observed. In normal conditions, this can increase predictability and veriﬁability since users can inspect the rule-set and observe state changes on-chain. Yet, when market conditions move outside the designers' assumptions, adjustment tends to occur through the mechanisms that the system already contains (e.g. parameter changes, upgrades, governance votes, or emergency procedures) rather than through References

real-time discretionary interventions. This matters because the range of possible responses is shaped by design. While automation reduces some forms of opacity and agency frictions, it also limits how quickly and in what ways the system can adapt. Furthermore, DeFi coordinates behaviour through crypto-economic design. Fees, rewards, penalties, collateral constraints, and issuance schedules are not merely technical parameters. They embody behavioural assumptions and shape strategic interaction among heterogeneous actors. Liquidity provision can be induced through fee revenues and token incentives and borrowing conditions can be governed by collateral ratios, liquidation penalties, and interest-rate curves. As the ecosystem matures, specialised participants (e.g. arbitrageurs, liquidators, and automated agents) learn to respond to these incentives at scale. Therefore, the performance of protocol-based intermediation depends on code correctness, but also on whether the incentive structure remains robust when participants optimise aggressively and when conditions change abruptly.

Furthermore, DeFi expands through modularity and composability. Protocols are often built as building blocks that plug into one another via shared standards and external services, enabling rapid innovation and interoperability (Schär, 2021 ). This architecture makes it possible to assemble new ﬁnancial products without vertically integrating all functions inside a single organisation. At the same time, it distributes intermediation across a network of dependencies like price feeds, liquidity conditions, bridges, and other components that are not managed by hierarchical oversight but by interfaces and shared conventions. Authority and accountability are therefore reconﬁgured. Even when governance is presented as decentralised, practical control can be exercised through upgrade processes, delegation structures, and privileged roles at the protocol or infrastructure layer. Understanding who can change parameters, deploy upgrades, or inﬂuence critical dependencies becomes necessary for interpreting how protocol-based ﬁnancial systems evolve and how they behave under stress.

## Chapter 2 **Traditional and Speciﬁc Risks of DeFi **

**Abstract ** A meaningful assessment of decentralized ﬁnance requires distinguishing between inherited ﬁnancial risks and those emerging from its architectural particularities. Market volatility, liquidity mismatches, and credit exposure persist, yet they operate within a markedly different institutional environment. Smart contracts, token-based governance, composability across protocols, and infrastructural interconnections introduce new channels through which fragility may arise and propagate. This chapter develops a structured reading of these risks, emphasizing how protocol design and systemic interlinkages alter both their manifestation and their transmission. The analysis establishes the conceptual scaffolding necessary for the empirical investigation that follows. \* \*

Recent studies agree that DeFi reproduces many risks of traditional ﬁnance but adds new ones or ampliﬁes some due to the technological and governance peculiarities of the protocols (Makarov et al., 2022 ). IOSCO ( 2023 ) highlights how DeFi is the result of a widening of information asymmetries and the absence of regulated intermediaries. In the following, we deﬁne the main theoretical risks identiﬁed in DeFi.

Furthermore, the anonymous/pseudonymous nature of transactions reduces the disciplining effects of reputation, potentially incentivizing excessive risk-taking compared to traditional markets. ## 3.1 **Stablecoins **

Stablecoins are cryptoassets designed to maintain a stable value against a reference, usually a single ofﬁcial currency such as the US dollar or the euro. Their deﬁning feature is the attempt to combine the technological infrastructure of cryptoassets (i.e., programmability, peer-to-peer transferability, and operation on distributed ledgers) with a relatively stable nominal value. In economic terms, they resemble privately issued monetary claims: holders expect to redeem them at par in the reference currency, and their acceptance depends on conﬁdence in that promise. Different stabilisation mechanisms generate different risk structures. The dominant category consists of reserve-backed stablecoins. In these arrangements, the issuer holds off-chain assets (e.g. bank deposits, short-term sovereign securities, or money market instruments) intended to back the tokens in circulation. Trust in the stablecoin is mediated by legal claims on these reserves and by attestations or audits rather than by on-chain collateral veriﬁcation. Other models rely on on-chain collateral, locking cryptoassets or tokenised ﬁnancial instruments in smart contracts to support issuance. In such cases, stability is linked to the market value of collateral and to the integrity of price oracles and liquidation mechanisms. Algorithmic designs, which attempt to stabilise value through endogenous supply adjustments or incentive schemes, rely on expectations about market behaviour and can be particularly vulnerable to sudden regime shifts. The European regulatory framework (MiCAR) reﬂects these distinctions by classifying single currency tokens as e-money tokens (EMTs) and broader asset-linked arrangements as asset referenced tokens (ARTs), with corresponding regulatory obligations (ECB, 2025; ESRB, 2025 ). The technical architecture of a stablecoin is thus inseparable from its legal and prudential treatment.

In terms of scale, stablecoins have grown rapidly and are highly concentrated. By September 2025, global market capitalisation was around USD 300 billion, representing roughly 7.5% of the overall cryptoasset market. Almost the entire market is denominated in US dollars, and two issuers (USDT and USDC) account for most of the outstanding supply (ESRB, 2025 ).

The economic function of stablecoins today is primarily internal to the crypto ecosystem. They serve as settlement assets on trading platforms, as collateral in derivatives markets, and as a unit of account in DeFi. By providing a relatively stable reference point in an environment characterised by high volatility, they facilitate leverage, margining, and liquidity provision. In decentralised exchanges and lending protocols, stablecoins are routinely paired with more volatile tokens, allowing participants to manage short-term price risk without exiting the crypto environment. This infrastructure role explains why stablecoins are central to crypto-market microstructure even if their use in retail commerce remains modest. Evidence on real-economy payment use conﬁrms this limited external usage. International monitoring indicates that most stablecoin transactions relate to trading or internal transfers within crypto platforms rather than to purchases of goods and services (IMF-FSB, 2024 ). External reports referenced in institutional assessments suggest that stablecoins account for a very small share of global e-commerce transactions and that central banks generally view their domestic use as marginal and concentrated in remittances or niche applications (ESRB, 2025 ). Stablecoins are therefore better described as a scalable private settlement layer for digital-asset markets than as a general-purpose payment instrument.

The main ﬁnancial vulnerability of stablecoins is run risk. Because holders expect redemption at par, conﬁdence plays a central role. If doubts arise about reserve adequacy, liquidity, or legal enforceability, redemption requests can rapidly increase. 3.1 Stablecoins

In reserve-backed models, this may force issuers to liquidate assets quickly, potentially at a discount. Depegging episodes (i.e. situations in which the market price of a stablecoin deviates materially from its reference value) demonstrate how rapidly conﬁdence can deteriorate. When a stablecoin is widely used as collateral, price instability can cascade into other markets. Liquidations in decentralised protocols can amplify downward pressure, while substitution into alternative stablecoins may produce opposite price movements. Such dynamics resemble classic liquidity spirals observed in traditional ﬁnancial markets, albeit within a technologically distinct environment (ESRB, 2025;  IMF, 2025 ).

Operational and governance risks compound these ﬁnancial vulnerabilities. In facts, stablecoin stability relies on the integrity of custodians, payment rails, smart contracts, and blockchain networks. Cyber incidents, outages, or failures in redemption processes can undermine conﬁdence even if reserves are adequate. Moreover, the broader crypto-technical stack (e.g., bridges and oracles) introduces additional points of fragility. Institutional assessments classify operational resilience and cyber risk alongside ﬁnancial stability concerns (ESRB, 2025 ). Stablecoins also pose challenges in the areas of ﬁnancial integrity and legal certainty. Their pseudonymous transferability and cross-border reach can facilitate illicit ﬁnance if AML/CFT controls are weak or unevenly applied. At the same time, regulatory fragmentation complicates enforcement. Differences in national regimes regarding custody, insolvency treatment, and redemption rights can create uncertainty about holders' legal position in stress scenarios (IMF-FSB, 2024;  IMF, 2025 ). For a money-like instrument, such uncertainty directly affects credibility. Effective regulation must therefore address not only reserve quality but also governance, transparency, and enforceable legal claims.

Regulatory responses have increasingly adopted a risk-based approach. International bodies emphasise the need for high-quality, liquid reserves, segregation of assets, clear and timely redemption rights, and credible recovery and resolution frameworks for arrangements that could become systemic (IMF, 2025 ). The objective is to align stablecoin oversight with the risks they generate, rather than to treat them as purely technological innovations. The G20 monitoring framework acknowledges that real-economy use remains limited but stresses that interconnections with mainstream ﬁnance may deepen (IMF-FSB, 2024 ).

Looking ahead, the relevance of stablecoins depends less on their current payment share and more on their potential scale and integration. Projections of market growth vary widely, reﬂecting regulatory uncertainty and evolving use cases (IMF, 2025 ). At present, stablecoins remain primarily instruments of crypto-market settlement rather than general money substitutes. However, if reserve portfolios continue to expand and cross-border usage intensiﬁes, their macroﬁnancial relevance could strongly increase. In that scenario, vulnerabilities linked to runs, reserve-market spillovers, operational fragility, and legal fragmentation would become more signiﬁcant.

## 3.2 **Crypto Asset Investment Products (CIPs) **

Crypto Asset Investment Products (CIPs) are ﬁnancial instruments designed to provide investors with exposure to cryptoassets without requiring direct ownership or technical management of the underlying tokens. Rather than holding private keys or interacting with blockchain protocols, investors access crypto markets through regulated vehicles structured under conventional ﬁnancial law. In this sense, CIPs represent a process of ﬁnancial intermediation and "securitisation" of crypto exposure, translating digitally native assets into formats compatible with traditional portfolio management and market infrastructures.

CIPs encompass a range of structures. They include exchange-traded products (ETPs), such as exchange-traded funds and exchange-traded notes referencing spot or derivative-based crypto exposures; open-ended and closed-end investment funds; index-tracking vehicles; structured notes and certiﬁcates; and other collective investment schemes with substantial cryptoasset allocations. Some CIPs incorporate leverage or derivative overlays to amplify exposure.

It is analytically useful to distinguish CIPs from two adjacent categories. The ﬁrst consists of crypto derivatives (i.e., futures, options, and swaps referencing cryptoassets) which transfer market risk through contractual payoffs and may entail margining obligations beyond the initial investment. The second comprises cryptorelated services provided by ﬁnancial institutions, such as custody, brokerage, asset management, and marketing of crypto-linked products. While these services do not necessarily involve balance-sheet exposure to cryptoassets, they generate operational, legal, and reputational risks that can transmit stress to the broader ﬁnancial system.

The market for CIPs expanded markedly from early 2024 onward, following regulatory developments in the United States, including the approval by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission of spot Bitcoin exchange-traded products. This decision catalysed both retail and institutional demand and encouraged established asset managers and ﬁnancial intermediaries to enter the market. By July 31, 2025, global capitalisation of crypto asset investment products exceeded USD 235 billion, compared with approximately USD 130 billion in December 2024. Although still modest relative to global equity and bond markets, these volumes are no longer negligible from a macroprudential perspective. At least six individual CIPs had market valuations above USD 5 billion as of mid-2025. The three largest products alone reached capitalisations of approximately USD 85 billion, USD 24 billion, and USD 21 billion respectively, collectively accounting for nearly half of total market capitalisation (ESRB, 2025 ). This concentration implies that idiosyncratic events affecting a limited number of vehicles could have system-wide repercussions within the CIP segment.

Market infrastructure is similarly concentrated. The three largest custodial providers manage approximately 39% of all CIPs and account for 60% of total product capitalisation. Concentration is even more pronounced among physically backed products, where the same three custodians service at least 63% of vehicles and hold around 71% of overall capitalisation (ESRB, 2025 ). CIPs function by replicating, directly or indirectly, the price performance of one or more crypto assets. In physically backed structures, the issuer acquires and holds the underlying cryptoassets, typically through specialised custodians, and issues shares or units that represent a proportional claim on those holdings. Investors trade these shares on regulated exchanges or subscribe and redeem them according to fund rules, without direct interaction with blockchain infrastructure. In derivative-based or synthetic structures, exposure is achieved through futures, swaps, or other ﬁnancial contracts referencing crypto assets. Leverage may be embedded either structurally or through portfolio techniques, increasing sensitivity to price ﬂuctuations.

From the investor's perspective, CIPs reduce operational barriers to entry. They eliminate the need to manage private keys, mitigate the risk of direct cyber theft, and integrate crypto exposure into conventional brokerage and custody accounts. From a systemic perspective, however, this intermediation transfers operational and custody risk from dispersed retail holders to centralised institutional actors. The integrity of private key storage, segregation of assets, and operational resilience of custodians becomes critical to market stability.

In the European Union, prudential treatment aims to constrain direct exposures of credit institutions. Many multifunctional groups (MFGs) operate across jurisdictions, often combining regulated and unregulated activities within the same corporate perimeter. Some maintain signiﬁcant operations outside the European Union; others engage in cryptoasset activities that remain only partially regulated or entirely outside existing supervisory frameworks. In certain cases, MFGs are primarily active in jurisdictions where activities that fall within the scope of MiCAR in the EU are not yet subject to equivalent regulatory standards. Moreover, even within the EU, some groups participate in activities related to unregulated cryptoassets, including crypto lending, ﬁnancing arrangements, or blockchain validation services.

From a microprudential perspective, the opportunities and risks associated with cryptoasset activities tend to be broadly comparable across different types of MFGs. However, the scale of operations, the degree of intra-group interdependence, the intensity of links with the broader crypto ecosystem, and the extent of connections with traditional ﬁnancial institutions can magnify these risks. When sufﬁciently large and interconnected, such exposures may acquire macroprudential relevance. The capacity of MFGs to combine issuance, trading, custody, and related services within integrated platforms can foster innovation, reduce transaction costs, and enhance user accessibility. Economies of scale, uniﬁed infrastructure, and rapid product development allow these groups to respond efﬁciently to evolving market demand.

At the same time, these structural characteristics generate vulnerabilities. Financial interdependencies within the group, operational concentration, cyber risks, governance weaknesses, conﬂicts of interest, and reputational spillovers can be ampliﬁed by centralisation and cross-functional integration. Complex corporate structures and cross-border regulatory fragmentation further complicate effective supervision, particularly where parent entities are established outside the EU. These factors underscore the importance of assessing MFG conﬁgurations when evaluating systemic risk and determining whether existing regulatory and supervisory frameworks remain adequate. In the followings, we discuss the most recurring risk scenarios.

## 3.3.1 ***Intra-Group Financing and Liquidity Risks ***

Within-group ﬁnancing difﬁculties, including liquidity problems, can generate risks for both MFGs and the broader market. MFGs, like other interconnected conglomerates, may depend on intra-group funding for their operations. Such dependencies can propagate risk within the group. For example, if a subsidiary experiences a signiﬁcant outﬂow of funds, perhaps to meet claims under stressed market conditions, or to pay a penalty or compensation due to a serious operational or conduct breach (i.e., a data breach or sanctions), other group entities may need to step in to support its strength. ## 3.3.2 ***Operational and Technological Risks ***

MFGs can be particularly vulnerable to operational risks, particularly technological and information technology risks, due to their heavy dependence on common or interconnected technological infrastructures. If the MFG handles large transaction volumes or values, an operational disruption can generate systemic risks. The concentration of infrastructure can cause an interruption or breach in an area to impact a wide range of services, assuming macroprudential relevance. For example, if central systems such as authentication servers or trading platforms were compromised, attackers could gain widespread access to the systems, with ripple effects (such as severe disruptions to crypto markets) that would extend beyond the MFG itself. Furthermore, the greater the market share of the MFG, the more it becomes a target for malicious actors' intent on stealing data or crypto assets or extorting a ransom (as in the case of the Bybit exchange attack, see Annex 2). Such actors can be both external and internal. Human errors can also have ampliﬁed effects due to the scale of the group's operations.

## 3.3.3 ***Governance Risks, Conﬂicts of Interest and Reputation ***

MFGs' structure and business models can pose risks related to governance challenges and conﬂicts of interest, which can result in reputational damage with contagion effects towards other entities perceived as similar. The vertical and horizontal integration of multiple crypto asset-related issuance services and/or activities under a single umbrella, often characterized by opaque governance, increases the likelihood that such challenges and conﬂicts will emerge. Poor customer interactions can quickly erode trust in the MFG group. This can affect the group itself and also the stability of the broader crypto market, if other entities are perceived as having similar problems.

## 3.3.4 ***Effects of Contagion and Loss of Conﬁdence ***

A loss of market conﬁdence in an MFG can trigger contagion. Reputational damage can lead to sudden withdrawals from platforms operating in similar markets, forcing the forced sale of crypto assets. This could destabilize not only the affected group, but also the crypto assets, platforms or interconnected services, propagating the risk of contagion.

## 3.3.5 ***Concentration and Market Power Risks ***

A signiﬁcant concentration of market power in an MFG can transform microprudential risks into macro-prudential risks. Centralization and concentration can create systemic vulnerabilities and increase the potential for market manipulation. For example, Binance not only operates one of the largest crypto asset exchanges in the world, but also controls the Binance Smart Chain (BSC), 1 over which it exerts signiﬁcant inﬂuence on network governance and validator selection. This vertical integration allows Binance to control different levels of the transactional process, raising concerns about market concentration and potential conﬂicts of interest. Globally, crypto asset markets show high levels of concentration, with few MFGs (nonbanks) dominating trading volumes. The Herﬁndahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) indicates that the crypto asset market was "highly concentrated" in 2023 (HHI \> 2500), with the market share of the ﬁve major exchanges steadily growing over the past ﬁve years. Recent analyses show that around 90% of trades are executed on the ten largest global exchanges, with Binance alone accounting for more than 50% of global trading volumes. The second largest exchange, UPbit, records only about a seventh of that volume. High levels of concentration are also observed within the EU, where 95% of transactions conducted on authorised CASPs in the EU are attributable to two platforms: Binance and Coinbase. Furthermore, Bitvavo and Kraken dominate the volume of euro-denominated exchanges. This concentration implies that any problems of such groups may have wider repercussions because of the scale of their operations. Similar concentration risks are also observed in the stablecoins market, with USDT issuer Tether Ltd representing a prime example of concentration risk, given its dominant role in providing liquidity-in addition to the wider risks arising from the lack of transparency on its reserves and the potential impact on market stability in the event of a loss of conﬁdence in USDT (ESRB, 2025 ).

From a supervisory point of view, the multifunctional nature of many Category 1 and Category 2a MFGs generates structural challenges. These groups often combine cryptoasset-related activities within complex and, at times, opaque corporate structures. Such opacity may hinder supervisors in identifying the relevant competent authorities and in obtaining a clear, consolidated view of risk exposures across the group. In some cases, groups may structure their operations in a way that exploits regulatory differences across jurisdictions, establishing key entities in locations characterised by weaker oversight or by the absence of industry-speciﬁc regulation, as illustrated by the FTX 2 case. ## 4.6 **Limitations **

Several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting the ﬁndings presented in this chapter. First, the analysis may be affected by survivorship bias. Protocols that have ceased operations following major incidents or have disappeared without formal reporting may be underrepresented in available datasets. As a result, the observable sample may disproportionately reﬂect projects that survived attacks or maintained sufﬁcient visibility to be documented, potentially underestimating the full extent of structural fragilities within the ecosystem. Second, underreporting of minor incidents represents a constraint. Smaller exploits, partial fund drains, or governance abuses are not always publicly disclosed, particularly where project teams seek to avoid reputational damage or user panic. In decentralised environments lacking mandatory disclosure standards, transparency depends largely on voluntary reporting, thirdparty investigations, or community monitoring. Consequently, the empirical database may understate the frequency of low-value or "silent" events, leading to a possible downward bias in incident counts. Third, the use of Total Value Locked (TVL) as an analytical benchmark entails methodological caveats. TVL is typically calculated on a mark-to-market basis and is therefore sensitive to token price ﬂuctuations, which may inﬂate or deﬂate perceived scale independently of underlying economic activity. Moreover, TVL captures on-chain locked assets but does not fully account for off-chain exposures, rehypothecation practices, or implicit leverage embedded in derivative or cross-protocol structures. As such, while TVL provides a useful aggregate indicator of ecosystem size and concentration, it should not be interpreted References

## as a comprehensive measure of systemic exposure. These limitations call for cautious

interpretation and highlight the need for improved data standardisation, disclosure practices, and integrated on- and off-chain monitoring tools in future research.

## 5.1 **Financial Market and Liquidity Risks **

This category includes cryptocurrency price volatility, risk of market collapses (rapid drawdowns), and liquidity problems in decentralized markets (thin order books, liquidity-concentrating AMM pools). These risks mirror traditional market risks but can be ampliﬁed by the lack of stabilization mechanisms (e.g., market makers of last resort) and the 24/7 non-stop nature of crypto markets. DeFi also includes run risks on key assets (e.g., redemption runs on algorithmic stablecoins) and the possibility of market manipulation through coordinated on-chain actions (orchestrated DEX pumps and dumps, oracle manipulation attacks that cause temporary price distortions, etc.). More in detail, on one hand, price volatility in cryptoassets is not merely higher in magnitude; it is also more persistent and more rapidly transmitted across interconnected markets through automated liquidation mechanisms and collateral dependencies. On the other hand, liquidity risk is structurally embedded in AMM models, where liquidity is endogenous and concentrated in speciﬁc pools rather than distributed across layered order books.

Some quantitative indicators can be employed to estimate and monitor these market and liquidity risks in a systematic manner. In particular, volatility-based and liquidity-sensitive measures allow for an assessment of both pure price risk and the additional losses that may arise under stressed trading conditions. The following indicators represent illustrative tools that can be used within a DeFi-speciﬁc risk-monitoring framework:

b ullet * * *Value at Risk * (VaR). VaR is a quantile-based risk measure that estimates the loss threshold that will not be exceeded over a given time horizon with a speciﬁed conﬁdence level (e.g., 99%), under the assumptions of the chosen model. VaR can be computed using different approaches, including parametric (often relying on distributional assumptions such as normality), historical simulation (non-parametric), or Monte Carlo simulation. It is widely used to summarise market (price) risk, but standard VaR typically does **not ** incorporate liquidity effects such as slippage and market impact. In the DeFi context, VaR can help assess the sensitivity of lending protocols, liquidity providers, and leveraged positions whose solvency depends on collateral values. For example, an increase in the VaR of a major collateral asset can act as an early-warning indicator of rising liquidation risk across lending platforms. Because DeFi protocols rely on predeﬁned collateralisation ratios, higher short-term volatility mechanically increases the likelihood of margin calls and forced liquidations. VaR can therefore be applied not only at the portfolio level but also at the protocol level to approximate stress thresholds under adverse market conditions. However, VaR alone may be insufﬁcient in decentralised markets, where liquidity can deteriorate abruptly and amplify realised losses, particularly in AMM-based environments, where slippage depends on pool depth and trade size and where liquidity may be concentrated or withdrawn rapidly in response to changing incentives (Heimbach & Huang, 2024 ). 5.2 Credit and Leverage Risks

b ullet * * *Liquidity-Adjusted Value at Risk * (L-VaR). This measure extends VaR to include liquidity risk. It combines the volatility of returns with that of the bid-ask spread. This indicator is especially pertinent in DeFi settings. By incorporating bid-ask spread dynamics or price impact functions into the risk estimate, L-VaR captures the additional loss that arises when positions must be sold off under stressed liquidity conditions. L-VaR in DeFi can be measured with on-chain metrics such as pool depth, concentration ratios within liquidity pools, historical slippage under large trades, and volatility of liquidity provision. For lending protocols, L-VaR can help quantify the combined effect of collateral price declines and declining market depth during liquidation shocks. For decentralised exchanges, it can signal vulnerability to sudden liquidity withdrawals or to coordinated market manipulation strategies. Because DeFi markets operate continuously without circuit breakers, liquidity stress can emerge and propagate rapidly, making liquidity-adjusted measures more informative than traditional volatility-based metrics alone. b ullet * * *Cost of Liquidity * (CoL). The CoL is the exogenous cost of liquidity, calculated as half of the relative bid-ask spread, multiplied by the liquidated position. It reﬂects how much it costs to exit a position under stressed market conditions. It is a direct indicator of liquidity risk. CoL peaks often correspond to trend reversals in the market, suggesting that it can be used as an early warning indicator for ﬁnancial instability. CoL acquires particular analytical relevance in decentralised ﬁnance, where liquidity provision is structurally endogenous and highly sensitive to market incentives. In DeFi, liquidity of AMM pools depends on the capital committed by liquidity providers, who can withdraw funds rapidly in response to several market conditions. Under speciﬁc circumstances, bid-ask spreads may suddenly widen and consequent slippage may increase non-linearly with trade size (Cartea et al., 2025 ). Measuring the CoL therefore provides a direct assessment of the cost of selling positions when liquidity deteriorates. Monitoring the evolution of CoL can allow supervisors or protocol designers to detect phases of tightening liquidity, particularly when increases in CoL are not solely attributable to higher volatility but to shrinking pool depth or liquidity concentration.

This category captures the risk of default and forced liquidation on DeFi lending platforms, as well as the ampliﬁcation effects associated with intensive leverage (including recursive borrowing and compounding strategies). In decentralised lending, credit risk is largely mechanised: collateral constraints and liquidation rules are executed by smart contracts and external liquidators. As a result, abrupt price moves can trigger synchronised liquidations, potentially reinforcing market stress through feedback effects and execution frictions. ## 5.3 **Operational and Technological Risks **

These collect all technical vulnerabilities of protocols and decentralized infrastructure. They are ideally divided into two subclasses:

(i) protocol code and logic vulnerabilities-bugs in smart contracts, logical errors in ﬁnancial formulas or access controls (protocol logic), incorrect implementations that can be exploited (such as reentrancy, integer overﬂow/underﬂow, bypassable signature checks); (ii) infrastructure and cybersecurity vulnerabilities-compromise of private keys (admin wallets or multi-sigs), attacks on cross-chain bridges (which often rely on validators or federated custodians), exploits on external services on which 5.3 Operational and Technological Risks

the protocol depends (oracle providers, APIs), and attacks on developers' or users' hardware/software platforms (supply chain attacks, malware, etc.).

This macro-category is peculiar to DeFi and ultimately represents the IT/cyber risks of a ﬁnancial system devoid of human intermediaries, where "code is law". The data shows that this includes both frequent but minor events (bug exploits on small protocols) and rare but devastating events (e.g. infrastructure hacks such as bridges with hundreds of millions of damages). ## 5.4 **Risks of Fraud and Unlawful Conduct **

This risk category encompasses all cases in which human actors exploit the DeFi ecosystem for fraudulent or abusive purposes. This category partially overlaps with operational risk. ## 5.6 **Interconnection, Contagion and Systemic Risks **

This category captures the possibility that localised shocks within DeFi may propagate through the system via cascading and contagion mechanisms. While systemic risk and ﬁnancial contagion are well-established concepts in traditional ﬁnance, the structural features of DeFi, its modular design, composability, high degree of asset interdependence, and global accessibility, create distinct transmission channels. Protocols are frequently layered on top of one another, share collateral assets, and rely on common liquidity pools, so that stress in one component may rapidly affect others. A triggering event, such as the exploitation of a foundational protocol or the sharp devaluation of a key asset, including a widely used stablecoin, can initiate a sequence of reactions across multiple platforms. Liquidation cascades in lending protocols, redemption runs, temporary or persistent peg deviations, and crossprotocol insolvencies are typical manifestations of such dynamics. Because smart contracts execute automatically and markets operate continuously, these feedback loops can unfold at high speed and without discretionary intervention. Concentration further ampliﬁes systemic exposure. A substantial share of DeFi collateral is often denominated in a limited set of assets, such as ETH, USDT, or wBTC. Heavy reliance on a small number of tokens increases the likelihood that distress affecting one asset will transmit across the broader ecosystem.

Furthermore, this risk category warrants distinct treatment within the taxonomy. Unlike operational or governance failures, systemic risk requires macroprudential monitoring tools capable of capturing network effects and aggregate vulnerabilities. Relevant instruments may include network-based stress testing, simulations of liquidation cascades, and indicators of aggregate leverage and collateral concentration.

The potential relevance of these dynamics extends beyond the boundaries of DeFi. International bodies have cautioned that, if sufﬁciently large and interconnected, decentralised markets could transmit shocks to traditional ﬁnance. Although, at present, DeFi and traditional ﬁnancial systems remain largely separated, ongoing integration, including the entry of banks of other ﬁnancial institutions into cryptorelated activities and the tokenisation of real-world assets, may increase cross-sector linkages. In that scenario, the mitigation of systemic risk within DeFi would assume greater importance for the resilience of the whole ﬁnancial system.

This revised taxonomy of DeFi risks offers an integrated framework that combines traditional ﬁnancial risk categories, adapted to a decentralised environment, with the operational, technological, governance, and conduct-related vulnerabilities speciﬁc to protocol-based systems. Such a mapping exercise provides a proposal basis for the design of targeted mitigation strategies and for the identiﬁcation of early-warning indicators capable of signalling emerging fragilities. Nevertheless, the proposed indicators are subject to important limitations. Many rely primarily on publicly observable on-chain data, which, despite its transparency, does not fully capture off-chain agreements, undisclosed governance arrangements, informal coordination among actors, or incidents that remain unreported. Metrics such as VaR, liquidity-adjusted measures, or concentration indices depend heavily on modelling assumptions and parameter calibration and may fail to capture extreme tail events in environments characterised by structural breaks and regime shifts. Network-based stress simulations, while informative, inevitably simplify the topology of inter-protocol dependencies and often abstract from behavioural feedback, endogenous liquidity withdrawals, or discretionary governance interventions. In addition, the rapid evolution of DeFi architectures implies that indicators calibrated on historical patterns may lose predictive power as new incentive schemes, cross-chain infrastructures, or attack vectors emerge.

These constraints suggest several directions for future research. First, greater effort should be devoted to integrating on-chain analytics with off-chain qualitative information, including governance documentation, audit quality, developer concentration, and legal structuring, in order to construct hybrid risk indicators. Second, the development of dynamic network models that incorporate behavioural responses, References

Third, empirical work could examine the predictive validity of proposed early-warning metrics by testing their ability to anticipate large-scale incidents or market-wide stress episodes. Fourth, comparative analysis between DeFi and traditional ﬁnancial contagion mechanisms may help reﬁne the "same activity, same risk" principle in a macroprudential context.

## 6.2 **Case Study: The Hack Ronin Bridge **

In recent years, one of the most alarming developments in crypto-related crime has been the sharp increase in funds stolen from DeFi protocols, with cross-chain bridges emerging as particularly vulnerable targets. A substantial share of these illicit ﬂows has been attributed to actors linked to North Korea, notably sophisticated groups such as the Lazarus Group. Estimates suggest that, in 2022 alone, North Korean-afﬁliated entities appropriated approximately USD 1.1 billion in cryptocurrency from DeFi platforms. As of mid-2022, cumulative losses from bridge-related exploits have been estimated at around USD 2 billion, underscoring the systemic relevance of this attack vector. 4

Cross-chain bridges present an attractive target for several structural reasons. First, they often concentrate signiﬁcant amounts of value within a limited number of smart contracts, effectively creating high-value honeypots. Second, their architecture is inherently complex: bridges typically involve on-chain components deployed on at least two distinct blockchains, combined with off-chain communication and validation mechanisms. This multi-layered structure expands the attack surface and increases the number of potential failure points, including validator compromise, message veriﬁcation ﬂaws, and governance weaknesses.

The attack on the Ronin Bridge, linked to the blockchain game Axie Inﬁnity, remains the most prominent example.

On 23 March 2022, approximately $625 million was illicitly extracted, making it one of the largest crypto thefts to date. Ronin was designed as a dedicated sidechain to Ethereum, intended to facilitate faster and cheaper in-game transactions. The Ronin Bridge enabled users to transfer assets such as ETH and USDC between Ethereum and the Ronin sidechain, thereby supporting the purchase of NFTs and in-game tokens. The bridge operated under a trusted validator model. Transaction approvals required signatures from at least ﬁve out of nine designated validator nodes. However, the majority of these validators were controlled by Sky Mavis, the company behind Axie Inﬁnity and Ronin, while another was operated by Axie DAO. This concentration of validation power signiﬁcantly weakened the intended decentralisation of the system. The Lazarus Group succeeded in compromising the private keys of ﬁve validators, four belonging to Sky Mavis and one associated with Axie DAO. The breach of the Axie DAO validator stemmed from a prior operational arrangement: Sky Mavis had obtained permission from Axie DAO to sign transactions on its behalf via a gas-free RPC node. This delegated access was never revoked. Once the attackers inﬁltrated Sky Mavis' internal systems, they were able to exploit this residual authorization and secure the ﬁfth required signature.

With control over ﬁve validator keys, the attackers effectively gained the ability to authorize fraudulent withdrawals. They subsequently transferred 173,600 ETH and 25.5 million USDC, amounting to roughly USD 625 million at the time of the exploit. In sum, the Ronin Bridge exploit reveals both the operational sophistication of the Lazarus Group and the structural weaknesses embedded in the security architecture of the protocol at the time of the attack. The subsequent laundering phase, fragmented across thousands of blockchain addresses and multiple networks, reﬂects a high degree of coordination and strategic planning. The progressive shift toward complex techniques such as chain hopping, particularly following the sanctions imposed on Tornado Cash, illustrates the rapid adaptive capacity of organised crypto-criminal networks.

These developments show how illicit actors adjust their operational models in response to regulatory interventions, repurposing tools originally designed for interoperability or privacy into instruments of obfuscation and capital recycling. The case therefore highlights the limits of isolated enforcement measures and the need for a more systemic approach to risk mitigation. Strengthened security governance, clearer allocation of operational responsibilities, improved transparency in validator and bridge architectures, and enhanced cross-border cooperation, both among protocols and between industry and regulatory authorities, are essential components of an effective response to money laundering and the illicit exploitation of blockchain infrastructures (Belenkov et al., 2025 ).

Moreover, the subsequent laundering phase highlights conduct and illicit-ﬁnance ampliﬁcation risks, whereby interoperable DeFi infrastructures are repurposed to facilitate large-scale obfuscation and capital recycling. The case is therefore analytically relevant because it demonstrates how different layers of risk, technical, organisational, ﬁnancial, and geopolitical, can converge within a single protocol architecture. In this sense, Ronin serves as a clear example of how vulnerabilities in governance design and infrastructural concentration may evolve into systemic events with cross-chain and cross-jurisdictional implications, fully consistent with the multidimensional risk framework developed in Chapter 5.

From a macroprudential perspective, the Ronin case highlights how infrastructures originally designed to enhance interoperability can become potential vectors of systemic vulnerability once they reach sufﬁcient scale and centrality. Cross-chain bridges operate as connective tissue within the DeFi ecosystem, linking liquidity pools, collateral structures, and user ﬂows across networks. When such nodes concentrate large volumes of value and depend on governance or validation mechanisms that are only nominally decentralised, their failure does not remain conﬁned to a single protocol. Instead, it may trigger broader effects through asset price adjustments, collateral liquidations, conﬁdence shocks, and disruptions in cross-chain settlement.

Although, at present, DeFi and traditional ﬁnance remain only partially interconnected, the progressive integration of institutional actors, tokenised real-world assets, and regulated intermediaries could amplify the systemic relevance of similar events in the future. In this evolving environment, the stability of key infrastructural components, bridges, custodial validators, liquidity hubs, acquires macroﬁnancial signiﬁcance. The Ronin exploit therefore anticipates a broader supervisory challenge: as DeFi architectures mature and scale, safeguarding ﬁnancial stability will require not only micro-level security enhancements but also macro-level monitoring of infrastructural concentration, governance centralisation, and cross-network interdependencies.

# Chapter 7 **Implications and Policy ** **Recommendations **

**Abstract ** The redistribution of intermediation across decentralized architectures carries signiﬁcant regulatory and policy implications. Institution-centered supervisory paradigms confront inherent limitations when applied to systems in which accountability is diffused across code, governance tokens, and infrastructural layers. This chapter formulates recommendations addressing reserve transparency, crossjurisdictional coordination, governance design, and data standardization. Rather than advocating prescriptive uniformity, it argues for adaptive oversight mechanisms calibrated to the structural properties of decentralized ﬁnancial arrangements. \* \*

## 7.1 **Stakeholder Implications and Mitigation Strategies **

In light of the incidents data and the proposed taxonomy, it is clear that a multilevel approach is necessary to identify risks early and mitigate their impact. Below, we outline key implications for various stakeholders in the DeFi ecosystem-users/ investors, developers/protocols, market participants-and propose strategies consistent with emerging practices, supporting them with quantitative metrics where possible.

## 7.1.1

***Continuous Monitoring and Data Analytics *** ***(for Protocols and Analysts) ***

Given how quickly exploits can occur, it is crucial to implement real-time on-chain activity surveillance systems to detect anomalies. Protocol teams and security ﬁrms should use algorithms that track early indicators of attack, such as abnormal fund movements (sudden massive exits from a liquidity pool; increasing token concentration in a few wallets); suspicious DEX trading patterns (volume spikes or volatility

on otherwise illiquid pairs); and unexpected use of ﬂash loans in protocols where they don't normally appear. These signals can anticipate a possible ongoing hack or imminent rugpull. For example, using ﬂash loans combined with operations on low-liquid assets could signal an attempt at price manipulation; a sudden shift of all tokens in a project to service addresses (e.g., mixers) could indicate that founders are performing an exit scam. In this sense, MEV bots can also act as both a threat and an indicator: repetitive transactions with very high gas to anticipate orders on DEXs suggest the presence of aggressive front-runners, a sign of possible arbitrage or manipulation opportunities taking place.

Supervisors and analytics providers are starting to develop dedicated dashboards: in the US, for example, the Treasury Department has emphasized the need to invest in tools for monitoring illicit risks in DeFi and sharing public-private information on emerging threats (FSB, 2023; Daian et al., 2019 ). A known challenge is the scarcity of reliable and comparable data: IOSCO highlights signiﬁcant information gaps in the DeFi ecosystem (pseudonym transactions, difﬁculty distinguishing retail vs. institutional users, etc.) (Makarov et al., 2022 ). Improving data collection appears necessary to strengthen risk monitoring capacity. This can be done by requiring major protocols to publish periodic metrics and audits of reserves, liquidity, and uses, as well as by promoting common on-chain reporting standards (e.g., smart contracts that expose key risk data via APIs). This increased transparency would help both regulators and advanced users identify risk situations before they escalate.

## 7.1.2

***Identifying Managers and Transparency in Governance *** ***(for Project Teams and Communities) ***

A unique problem with DeFi is the difﬁculty of identifying responsible parties to whom regulatory obligations or corrective actions can be attributed, given that many protocols claim to be "decentralized" and without leadership. There are core developers and sometimes legal entities (e.g. foundations) behind each project. IOSCO recommends that authorities require DeFi projects to clearly state roles and responsibilities behind protocols, even when formally decentralized (Werbach, 2018 ).

While awaiting regulatory requirements, it is in the protocols' interest to improve trust by transparently communicating their governance: for example, maintaining public records of core developers, validators, or core node operators, and documenting on-chain decision-making processes (who proposes and votes on changes). More transparent governance open to public scrutiny helps mitigate operational risks and conﬂicts of interest. If a protocol communicates a critical change to risk parameters (collateral factor, yield) in advance, users can react and report any critical issues before implementation. Many DeFi projects have introduced security mechanisms into governance, such as quorum requirements and minimum voting times to avoid 7.1 Stakeholder Implications and Mitigation Strategies

hasty decisions, or the establishment of security committees with limited powers to pause smart contracts in the event of a full-blown exploit.

It is important for community stakeholders to support such measures and demand high standards of effective decentralization: for example, preferring protocols with distributed multisigs and timelocks for updates, avoiding projects where a developer controls administrative keys alone. Future empirical studies may conﬁrm that protocols with more decentralized and transparent governance suffer fewer incidents.

## 7.1.3

***Improving Technological Resilience (for Developers *** ***and Auditors) ***

From a technical perspective, there are several strategies for mitigating exploit risks. A well-established best practice is to conduct independent code audits: having smart contract code reviewed by third-party security companies can identify common vulnerabilities before launch. However, as previously discussed, audits do not guarantee full coverage, especially when faced with new ﬁnancial logic or additions with external oracles. For protocols that handle large sums or critical functions, the use of formal checks (mathematical proofs of code correctness) would be desirable at least for the most sensitive modules, for instance, collateral management contracts or Net Asset Value calculations of algorithmic stablecoins.

Regulators and institutional investors may in the future demand security certiﬁcations as a prerequisite for interacting with certain protocols (similar to how banks and funds today require IT audit certiﬁcates from suppliers). For example, the EU with MiCA will require signiﬁcant token issuers and crypto service providers to take steps to ensure risk-proportioned cybersecurity. Alongside audits, it is crucial to incentivize responsible bug discovery: bug bounty programs like Immuneﬁ 1 have already helped prevent serious vulnerabilities by rewarding hackers "white hat". In parallel, the DeFi community could beneﬁt from the creation of a sort of "Underwriters Laboratory" for smart contracts, which is an independent body that conducts standardized robustness tests on widely used contracts (e.g., ERC-20 templates, AMM implementations, lending modules) and publishes a security rating. This would provide a public and unbiased reference on the quality of projects' code, helping users distinguish well-built protocols from improvised and potentially insecure forks.

Finally, on the protocol side, it is advisable to implement algorithmic limitations and emergencies: for example, circuit breakers or automatic limits that trigger in the event of anomalous conditions (liquidity drying, price excursions \> X%, etc.), temporarily freezing some operations. Some large DeFi protocols have begun to integrate these logics (e.g. Aave has daily borrow limits for risky assets). Such measures add a layer of security, although they introduce latency and temporary "centralization", a trade-off necessary in the face of lightning threats.

## 7.1.4 ***Investor Education and Tools ***

DeFi users, especially retail and institutional users, face a higher level of risk and complexity than traditional ﬁnance. It is therefore essential to strengthen ﬁnancial and digital education initiatives focused on crypto and DeFi: investors should be aware of the main scam schemes (rugpull, phishing, etc.) and the warning signs to observe before interacting with a protocol. Some red ﬂags empirically associated with fraudulent projects include: promises of extremely high returns compared to the market average (e.g., pools offering \> 1000% APY on common assets-often a prelude to Ponzi schemes) (Chen et al., 2020 ); lack of open-source code or public audits; totally anonymous and/or unveriﬁed teams; tokenomics that allocate a huge share of tokens to the team/founders; deposit locking mechanisms that are too restrictive or unclear. Investors should diversify and never allocate more than they are willing to lose into individual high-risk DeFi protocols. For institutional or professional investors entering DeFi, it is advisable to adopt a risk-management approach: use on-chain analytics tools to monitor a protocol's health indicators in real time (TVL, collateral usage, default rates, abnormal activity), use protocol rating services (some startups are starting to offer them), and demand clear contractual conditions (for example, preferring protocols with integrated insurance, or with circuit breaker 2 clauses as above). In the future, standard metrics (e.g., DeFi Sharpe ratio) could emerge to compare risk/return on DeFi strategies, or protocol solvency scores calculated by third parties. This would greatly help users navigate the landscape.

## 7.1.5 ***Risk Insurance and Mutualization ***

A growing mitigation mechanism is decentralized hacks insurance. There are protocols (Nexus Mutual, InsurAce, etc.) that offer coverage by compensating users in the event of a loss of funds due to smart contract exploits or bugs. These solutions replicate the traditional insurance function: capital pools collect premiums from policyholders and pay claims when covered hacks occur, with patterns ranging from mutualistic to parametric insurance (e.g., policies that pay if a stablecoin loses its peg beyond certain percentage points). Adopting insurance can reduce the ﬁnal impact for affected investors and indirectly incentivize virtuous behaviour (companies often require audits and minimum standards from the protocol to be insured, acting as certiﬁers). Currently, penetration of these tools remains limited compared to the overall DeFi market, partly due to insufﬁcient insurance capital to cover a systemic collapse.

Policy initiatives could be envisaged to strengthen this layer of risk absorption. Tax or regulatory incentives might encourage DeFi operators to obtain coverage or contribute to sector-speciﬁc mutual funds. A useful analogy can be drawn with traditional ﬁnancial infrastructures, where clearing or guarantee funds (e.g., interbank

However, bodies such as the BIS have suggested the idea of incorporating prudential rules directly into protocols ("embedding supervision in code") and setting up backstops for critical functions such as stablecoins (Bank of England, 2022 ).

Looking ahead, it is not out of the question that central banks or public consortia could provide last-resort liquidity facilities for segments of the crypto market (e.g., systemic stablecoins), provided that such protocols adhere to rigorous security standards-an analogy to the lender of last resort role in the banking system. The strategies described above highlight that no single measure is decisive: a coordinated set of technological solutions, market measures, and user precautions is needed to effectively mitigate DeFi risks. For example, the widespread adoption of audits and bug bounties 3 may reduce common technical exploits, but it will not eliminate the risks of fraudulent conduct; similarly, sophisticated on-chain monitoring systems may detect ongoing attacks, but it is still necessary to prevent funds from ﬂowing indiscriminately to untrustworthy projects (education and disclosure issues). Ultimately, all ecosystem players-developers, users, crypto intermediaries, authorities-have shared responsibilities in building a more secure and resilient DeFi environment.

## 7.2 **Regulatory and Policy Recommendations **

Supervisory authorities worldwide are increasingly confronted with the challenge of integrating DeFi within established standards of ﬁnancial security and market integrity, while avoiding regulatory responses that might unnecessarily constrain technological innovation. In this context, IOSCO ( 2023 ) has articulated nine highlevel recommendations aimed at framing a coherent regulatory approach to DeFi. A central pillar of this framework is an activity-based approach grounded in the principle of technological neutrality. Regulators are encouraged to analyse DeFi products and services according to their economic function and risk proﬁle, rather than their technological form, in line with the maxim " *same activity, same risk, same * *regulatory outcome* ". Under this logic, blockchain-based arrangements that replicate traditional activities such as deposit-taking, credit intermediation, or collective investment management, should, where appropriate, be subject to regulatory obligations comparable to those applicable in traditional ﬁnance. A related objective concerns the containment of regulatory arbitrage. DeFi should not evolve into a

regulatory vacuum exploited to circumvent existing legal standards. Where decentralised architectures replicate the features of regulated ﬁnancial instruments such as derivatives or portfolio management services, existing laws should be applied, and legal responsibility should be attached to identiﬁable economic actors involved in the design, governance, or operation of the protocol (Werbach, 2018 ). Several jurisdictions are therefore exploring mechanisms to attribute accountability to developers, core teams, or key holders behind ostensibly decentralised systems, with a view to ensuring enforceability of anti-fraud, market abuse, and investor protection rules.

Transparency and disclosure requirements constitute another essential dimension. Reducing information asymmetries, particularly for retail users, requires DeFi projects to provide clear and comprehensive disclosures regarding governance structures, audit status of smart contracts, embedded risks, and potential conﬂicts of interest (Werbach, 2018 ). In practice, this could entail the introduction of standardised risk warnings within protocol interfaces, including explicit statements on the absence of deposit insurance or the presence of unaudited code, mirroring disclosure practices common in traditional ﬁnancial markets. Given the inherently cross-border nature of DeFi, effective supervision also depends on enhanced international cooperation. Coordinated enforcement actions, intelligence sharing on fraud and scams, and convergence toward common regulatory standards are necessary to prevent malicious actors from relocating to more permissive jurisdictions. International bodies such as the FSB and IOSCO are promoting such coordination, but meaningful implementation ultimately depends on the commitment and legislative action of individual countries.

A concrete regulatory development is the European MiCAR (Markets in Cryptoassets) regulation, approved in 2023, which introduces uniform rules for cryptoasset issuance and services in the EU. MiCAR speciﬁcally covers stablecoins (distinguishing between asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens) and various services such as trading, custody, and cryptoasset consulting (European Union, 2023 ). However, MiCAR explicitly excludes from its perimeter truly decentralized activities without identiﬁable intermediaries (for example, totally permissionless token issuance). This leaves a regulatory grey area: many DeFi applications will initially escape the MiCAR regime until there is an identiﬁable legal entity "promoter" or service provider. For example, a pure AMM with no parent company does not fall under MiCAR; however, if a centralized exchange offers access to tokens generated by that AMM, it may have to comply with transparency requirements for those tokens. ESMA has indicated that it will issue guidelines on aspects such as cryptoasset staking, DeFi lending, etc., to bridge some gaps in the meantime. Looking ahead, a MiCAR 2.0 that explicitly addresses DeFi is possible, but in the meantime, it will be crucial to encourage forms of self-regulation in the industry: independent audits, proof-of-reserves, and public on-chain asset registries are all practices that industry can voluntarily adopt to demonstrate reliability and cooperate with regulators.

Another area of regulatory focus is stablecoins and stores of value in the DeFi system. The collapse of TerraUSD demonstrated the potential systemic impact of an unsecured algorithmic stablecoins. MiCAR already has stringent requirements for signiﬁcant stablecoins (in terms of capital, reserves, mandatory audits) but only if References

there is an identiﬁed central issuer. For decentralized stablecoins or algorithms, ad hoc interventions or inclusion in future regulatory packages will likely be needed. Some experts propose the idea of certifying stablecoins algorithms or placing limits on the allowed algorithmic leverage (e.g., requiring minimal collateralization). Furthermore, the possibility of integrating security "guardrails" at the protocol level is discussed: the BIS suggested that some prudential controls could be directly encoded in smart contracts (e.g., self-regulating risk parameters) to prevent certain ex ante excessively risky behaviors (Makarov et al., 2022; Werbach, 2018 ).

On the enforcement side, authorities are stepping up efforts to prosecute crypto crimes. Coordinated action, as against the Lazarus Group (see Chapter 6 ), indicates that large-scale DeFi attacks are now considered a threat to international security. Financial cybercrime units specializing in blockchain must therefore be strengthened, promoting collaboration between agencies (law enforcement, ﬁnancial intelligence) and with private actors (exchanges that can freeze stolen funds, analytics ﬁrms that track ﬂows). The goal is to make the risk of legal consequences more tangible even for hackers operating through DeFi. In parallel, the adequacy of the existing regulatory framework needs to be assessed: for example, anti-money laundering legislation will likely need to extend to include DEXs and smart contracts in some way, perhaps requiring front-ends or aggregators to integrate AML controls for access to certain services.

Ultimately, the regulatory recommendations converge on several key points: ﬁlling the regulatory gap by applying existing laws to DeFi where possible; imposing principles of prudence and transparency (disclosures, conﬂict management, audit requirements) similar to those in the ﬁnancial sector; and promoting international convergence to avoid arbitrage. Authorities must, however, balance intervention with the need to avoid stiﬂing innovation: overregulation could push DeFi operations completely into the shadows or into unregulated platforms abroad, increasing rather than reducing systemic risks. One desired path is to co-create solutions: involving the DeFi community in setting standards (e.g., certiﬁcations, security best practices) and possibly providing regulatory sandboxes where new on-chain compliance models can be tested (such as embedded supervision projects that use smart contracts to automate regulatory reporting).

# Closing Remarks

The analysis conducted highlighted how decentralized ﬁnance represents both an opportunity for innovation and a source of signiﬁcant risks to the stability and integrity of the ﬁnancial system. The proposed updated taxonomy allowed us to more precisely distinguish the different types of vulnerabilities, from market and credit risks to operational, governance, and systemic risks, showing how they intertwine in a highly interconnected and rapidly evolving ecosystem. Empirical data conﬁrm the coexistence of two complementary dynamics: on the one hand, a multitude of small but recurring incidents, often linked to widespread fraud and minor technical vulnerabilities; on the other, a smaller number of exceptional events, characterized by catastrophic losses and potential systemic contagion effects. This asymmetry requires differentiated mitigation approaches: widespread protection of retail investors against small-scale scams, but also macroprudential and regulatory tools to prevent and contain systemic risks.

A further critical element that has emerged concerns the ambivalent role of mixers and other pseudo-anonymous infrastructures, which, while responding to legitimate privacy needs, today constitute a privileged channel for laundering illicit proceeds, drastically reducing the possibility of recovering stolen funds. This makes strengthening international cooperation, adopting advanced on-chain tracking techniques, and developing innovative regulatory tools that balance security and conﬁdentiality protection essential.

DeFi does not eliminate ﬁnancial risk. It reconﬁgures its sources, transmission channels, and accountability structures. Understanding this reconﬁguration is the precondition for designing proportionate, technologically informed, and forwardlooking regulatory responses. In conclusion, the evolution of DeFi poses unprecedented challenges for policymakers, investors, and market participants. Only an integrated approach, combining technology surveillance, risk management strategies, more transparent governance mechanisms and a globally harmonised regulatory framework, can make it possible to leverage the beneﬁts of innovation without compromising ﬁnancial stability and user conﬁdence. ## Attacks Deﬁnitions

## Access Control Exploit: An exploit in which an attacker exploits incorrect or absent

conﬁgurations in the access control mechanisms of a DeFi smart contract, gaining unauthorized privileges, modifying the contract state, or performing restricted functions, often due to a lack of Ownable, onlyOwner, require() modules, or poorly managed roles (Zhou et al., 2023).

## Address Poisoning: A phishing attack that exploits the address shortening mech

anism in Web3 platforms (e.g. Etherscan, MetaMask) to insert false addresses into the victim's transaction history, causing them to transfer funds to a malicious address very similar to a legitimate one (Guan et al., 2024).

## API Key unauthorized access: An attack in which an attacker gains unauthorized

access to the functionality of a wallet, smart contract, or DeFi infrastructure by exploiting an exposed, mishandled, or compromised API key (Li et al., 2022).

## API Signature Exploit: An exploit that allows an attacker to generate valid API

signatures without knowing the shared secret key, exploiting structural weaknesses in the signature algorithm (e.g. MD5), such as the length-extension attack (Duong et al., 2009).

## Arbitrary External Call: A situation in which a software system (e.g., smart

contract, control module, algorithm) makes calls to uncontrolled or unveriﬁed external components, introducing arbitrary noise, unpredictable behavior, or security vulnerabilities, similar to the concept of external arbitrary noise in the statistical model (Senov et al., 2014).

## Arbitration Exploit: Malicious behavior that exploits economic arbitrage or

protocol mechanisms in a manner not intended or authorized by the system, with the aim of obtaining unfair advantage, illicit proﬁts, or market manipulation (Li et al., 2022).

## Balance Disparity Exploit: An attack technique in which an individual exploits

temporary or structural imbalances in the balance sheets of smart contracts or liquidity pools to gain unfair economic beneﬁts, often bypassing the protection mechanisms provided by the protocol (Chen et al., 2023). Appendix A: Attack Methods

Bonding Curve Exploit: An attack technique that exploits vulnerabilities or asymmetries in the automated token pricing function, based on bonding curves, to generate unfair proﬁt (Qian et al., 2023).

Borrow Logic Exploit: An attack technique in which a malicious actor exploits logical ﬂaps in the lending process of a DeFi protocol, such as collateral management, checking loan terms, or interacting with smart contracts, to obtain assets unauthorizedly or unfairly (Qian et al., 2023).

Burn Function Mistake: A design or security ﬂaw in a smart contract's burn() function that allows unauthorized destruction of tokens, or fails to properly update totalSupply, resulting in economic imbalances or vulnerabilities (Qian et al., 2023).

Buy Function Exploit: An attack that targets the buy() function of a DeFi contract, exploiting poorly formed inputs, lack of price controls, or insecure dependencies (e.g., oracles, rewards, external callbacks) to incorrectly obtain tokens at favorable conditions (Qian et al., 2023).

Calculation Error in Liquidation Engine: A logical, computational, or design error within the automated liquidation system of a DeFi protocol, leading to incorrect, unjustiﬁed, or missed liquidations.

Calldata underﬂow: An error that occurs when a contract receives less data than expected in the calldata structure, resulting in unexpected memory accesses, validation bypasses, or uninitialized variable reads (Shou et al., 2024).

CEX Price Manipulation Attack: A strategy in which an attacker temporarily manipulates the price of an asset on a centralized platform (CEX), knowing that that price data will be used by a DeFi protocol through an oracle, to obtain unfair proﬁts or activate automated behaviors (liquidations, swaps, collateral updates) (Xiong et al., 2024).

Claim Contract Flashloan Exploit: An attack technique in which a malicious actor exploits the claim (request for rewards or beneﬁts) function of a DeFi contract, temporarily manipulating the protocol state through a ﬂash loan within a single transaction. This allows you to simulate artiﬁcial eligibility conditions, receive the expected reward, and restore the original state by returning the ﬂash loan, bypassing contract controls (Wu, 2024).

Cloudﬂare Key Compromised: A security incident in which a private key (or critical token) managed through the Cloudﬂare infrastructure is exposed, stolen, or misused. This can compromise the integrity of the HTTPS connection, enable manin-the-middle attacks, allow decryption of TLS trafﬁc, or allow unauthorized access to systems dependent on that key (Bhargavan et al., 2017).

Collateral Offboarding Mistake: A logical, operational, or governance error in the removal (offboarding) process of a collateral asset supported by a DeFi protocol. It can cause forced devaluation, unintentional liquidation, or loss of funds by users who still hold positions based on such collateral (Aspris, 2025).

Collateral Validation Exploit: An attack that exploits weaknesses in the collateral veriﬁcation (validation) logic in a DeFi protocol. When the smart contract does not adequately control the legitimacy, value, or provenance of the collateral deposited, an attacker can simulate valid assets to obtain undeserved loans, rewards, or privileges (Aspris, 2025). Appendix A: Attack Methods

Contract not initialized: A vulnerability condition that occurs when a smart contract is deployed on the blockchain without properly initializing critical variables, state structures, or external modules (such as proxies or oracle bindings) (Zhou et al., 2023).

Controlled Rebalance Manipulation: A form of attack in which an actor, through direct or indirect control of funds and wallets within a DeFi protocol with automated rebalancing mechanisms, artiﬁcially alters the distribution of assets in the pool, inducing fraudulent economic beneﬁts such as positive slippage, fund drainage, or exchange price manipulation.

Cross Chain Multisig Deployment Exploit: An attack that targets multisig (multisignature) protocols distributed across multiple blockchains, exploiting inconsistencies between security, quorum, and addressing conﬁgurations across different networks. It allows the attacker to bypass authorization checks, duplicate the signature on a different chain, or manipulate the signature logic in cross-chain environments (Harris, 2023).

CTX Exploit: Vulnerability that exploits inefﬁcient or insecure handling of Crossshard Transactions (CTXs) in fragmented (sharded) blockchain protocols. Attack occurs when an actor manipulates the selection, distribution, or conﬁrmation of CTXs, often through incentivized brokers or rebalancing mechanisms, to extract value, bypass atomicity rules (Chen et al., 2024).

Database Attack: A category of exploits in which an attacker inserts or manipulates malicious code that is subsequently saved, interpreted, or executed within a database. These attacks may include SQL injection, second-order injection, or structural manipulations that compromise database integrity and security.

Debt Swap Exploit: A DeFi protocol attack strategy that leverages the settlement of undercollateralized loans using ﬂashswaps, manipulating exchange rates between debt and collateral assets to achieve higher proﬁts than conventional settlement strategies (Yaish et al., 2023).

DebtManager Exploit: An attack technique that exploits logical or design vulnerabilities in the debt management module ("Debt Manager") of a DeFi protocol. The attacker manipulates functions such as settlement, repayment, collateral calculation, or debt tracking, often through secondary or unprotected methods, to obtain undue proﬁts or circumvent solvency controls (CertiK, 2024).

Decimal Miscalculation Exploit: A vulnerability in smart contracts that exploits inconsistencies in the treatment of decimals, particularly errors in scaling factors, conversions between tokens with different accuracies (e.g., 6 vs. 18 decimals), or imperfect rollovers, to manipulate the value of assets, loans, or collateral. This exploit can lead to the unintentional creation of excess tokens, distortion of economic valuations in DeFi protocols, and misappropriation of funds, without violating formal contract rules (Hu et al., 2023).

Delegatecall Exploit: An exploit in which an attacker leverages Solidity's delegatecall function to execute external code while maintaining the storage context of the calling contract, gaining unauthorized access to critical variables, modifying the contract state, or performing malicious functions, often in proxy or upgradeable environments (Zhou et al., 2023). Appendix A: Attack Methods

Deployer Wallet Compromised: A vulnerability in which the wallet that deployed a smart contract is breached, allowing third parties to make unauthorized changes, steal funds, or manipulate critical protocol functions (Carter et al., 2021).

Deposit Function Exploit: Vulnerabilities in which the deposit function (deposit()) in a DeFi protocol is manipulated or exploited to obtain excess assets, alter the internal state of the contract, or circumvent economic constraints, often through decimal errors, atomic interactions, or lack of validation (Zhou et al., 2023).

Dev Privilege Oversight Exploit: Vulnerabilities in DeFi protocols where developer wallets or deployers with special privileges are not properly revoked, audited, or decentralized, allowing for potential unauthorized contract or protocol manipulation (Carter et al., 2021).

DNS Spooﬁng: Attacks in which DNS infrastructure is manipulated to redirect the user to a malicious version of the front-end or other external services. It can lead to key or asset theft (Zhou et al., 2023).

Donation Attack: Vulnerabilities in which an attacker artiﬁcially modiﬁes the ﬂow of funds to a contract, for example, by sending tokens without going through ofﬁcial functions, to manipulate internal state, alter accounting logic, or achieve cost-free proﬁts by exploiting the fact that the contract calculates assets via balanceOf rather than internal accounting (Kong et al., 2025).

Drain vaults: Exploits in which an attacker drains funds held in a DeFi vault by abusing privileged logic, unsafe withdrawal mechanisms, or other contract-level ﬂaws that enable unauthorized token outﬂows, thereby undermining the protocol's economic security (Su et al., 2023).

By manipulating asset prices through unsecured oracles or interfaces, the attacker can execute favorable transactions, such as opening and closing positions with artiﬁcial values, resulting in illicit proﬁts (Wu et al., 2021).

Voting Power Inﬂation: A phenomenon whereby voting power in a decentralized governance system is artiﬁcially increased or concentrated, often through the acquisition of governance tokens, compromising fairness and the democratic distribution of decisions (Barbereau et al., 2022).

Vyper Compiler Bug: Bug in the Vyper compiler (versions 0.2.15, 0.2.16, and 0.3.0) that assigned incorrect memory slots to reentrancy locks, allowing functions marked as @nonreentrant to still be re-invoked at runtime. This exposed smart contracts to vulnerable intermediate states, facilitating reentrancy attacks.

Withdraw Function Argument Manipulation: An attack that exploits the manipulation of withdraw() function arguments in a smart contract. If parameters (such as recipient or amount) are not properly validated, an attacker can alter the logic of the function to withdraw funds from other addresses or obtain amounts greater than the authorized balance (Qin et al., 2021).

WithdrawStuckToken() exploit: An exploit that abuses emergency functions like withdrawStuckToken() to withdraw funds from the contract, often without distinction between locked assets and user deposits. If access is not restricted or tracked, it can turn into an authorized rug pull (Qin et al., 2021).
